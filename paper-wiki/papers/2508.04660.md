# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** an LM program $\Phi$ composed of a set of modules $\mathcal{M} = \{M_1, \ldots, M_{|\mathcal{M}|}\}$, each with its own prompt template $\pi_M$ and LM weights $\theta_M$, orchestrated by control flow that routes structured inputs and outputs between modules (e.g., a query-generation module feeding a retriever feeding a response-generation module); a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x, m)\}$ of program inputs and optional metadata; and a program-level reward function $\mu(y, \rho, m)$ scoring an execution's final output $y$ and its trajectory of module calls $\rho$.
* **Output (Y):** MMGRPO, a multi-module generalization of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) that updates each module's own LM weights from grouped, module-level rollout comparisons rather than treating the whole program as one undifferentiated policy call, released as the `dspy.GRPO` optimizer in the open-source DSPy library.
* **Core problem:** GRPO was designed for single-stage settings — one prompt, a group of same-prompt completions, one policy being updated — but modern LM-based systems are increasingly modular programs mixing several distinct LM calls, each with its own prompt template and role, plus non-LM tools. It is not established how to extend GRPO's grouped-advantage machinery to this multi-module setting, where different rollouts of the same program-level input can diverge in the number of steps taken, which modules are invoked, and in what order — due to control-flow branching or early termination from failures such as parsing errors.
* **Scope:** a first, deliberately simple baseline design (module-level grouping) rather than an exhaustive search over possible multi-module RL formulations; validated on three LM programs (a single-module classifier, a two-module privacy-delegation pipeline, a two-module four-hop retrieval-augmented claim verifier) using two 8B open-weight LMs (llama3.1-8b-instruct, qwen3-8b), explicitly not tested at larger model scale or with full-parameter (non-LoRA) fine-tuning.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The paper does not claim to originate GRPO (Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath), prompt optimization for LM programs (MIPROv2, Opsahl-Ong et al. 2024; DSPy's own rejection-sampling optimizers, Khattab et al. 2024), or combining weight and prompt optimization in general — BetterTogether (Soylu et al. 2024) already showed this combination helps, but only for offline RL via rejection fine-tuning on outcome-filtered trajectories. Its specific novelty, stated directly in the introduction, is being "the first implementation of GRPO that applies to sophisticated pipelines of LMs": prior multi-turn RL formulations (Search-R1, ReTool, RAGEN-style approaches cited elsewhere in this wiki) expand a single LM's prompt serially across turns, whereas an arbitrary LM program's control flow determines what context each module sees by explicitly selecting its inputs — a structurally different problem, since a module such as PAPILLON's privacy-preserving delegation call is deliberately prevented from seeing the full interaction history. This motivates a genuinely new multi-module formulation rather than reusing existing multi-turn RL machinery, and lets the paper run the first controlled three-way comparison of prompt optimization alone, online multi-module RL alone, and their staged composition (BetterTogether extended to the online-RL setting) on the same LM programs.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If GRPO's requirement for a shared prompt within a group is relaxed by instead grouping rollouts at the module level — aligning structurally comparable module calls across different program-trajectory rollouts by module identity and relative order of invocation, rather than requiring identical inputs or a fixed trajectory structure — then GRPO-style policy-gradient updates can be applied independently to each module's own weights using the program-level outcome reward as uniform credit, without requiring shared histories, intermediate supervision, or a redesigned single-policy training signal. The paper further hypothesizes that this online-RL approach is complementary to (not a replacement for) automatic prompt optimization, such that staging prompt optimization first and then applying mmGRPO to the resulting prompt-optimized program (following the offline BetterTogether recipe, now applied online) should outperform either method alone, because prompt optimization produces higher-quality initial rollouts that give mmGRPO a stronger training signal early in training.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Weight optimization for LMs.** PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) as the RLHF-era standard; DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) as a contrastive-preference alternative avoiding explicit reward modeling; GRPO (Shao et al. 2024) as the value-free, group-normalized-advantage method this paper directly extends. See this wiki's [GRPO](../concepts/grpo.md) concept page for other wiki papers applying GRPO to single-policy agent settings.
* **Prompt optimization.** Instruction-generation approaches (Yang et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023; Fernando et al. 2024's Promptbreeder); gradient-based discrete-prompt optimization (Shin et al. 2020; Wen et al. 2023); RL-based prompt optimizers (RLPrompt, Deng et al. 2022); MIPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong et al. 2024) as the specific state-of-the-art Bayesian-optimization-based prompt optimizer used as this paper's PO baseline and BetterTogether's first stage.
* **Optimizing LM programs' prompts and weights jointly.** DSPy (Khattab et al. 2024) as the framework this paper's optimizer is released inside, originally built around rejection-sampling-based prompt optimization; BetterTogether (Soylu et al. 2024) as the direct methodological precedent this paper extends from offline rejection-fine-tuning RL to online policy-gradient RL.
* **Benchmarks used as LM-program testbeds.** Banking77 (Casanueva et al. 2020, single-module intent classification); PUPA/PAPILLON (Siyan et al. 2024, privacy-preserving delegation, two modules); HoVer (Jiang et al. 2020, many-hop claim verification built on HotPotQA, Yang et al. 2018, two modules iterated over four hops with a ColBERTv2 retriever, Santhanam et al. 2021).
* **Key people and affiliations.** Multi-institution author list spanning University of Notre Dame, Stanford University, UC Berkeley, MIT, Databricks, Contextual AI, Normal Computing, and Zoom, Inc.; three authors (Ziems, Soylu, Agrawal) are marked equal first-contribution. Omar Khattab (MIT) and Christopher Potts (Stanford) are DSPy's original creators; this is effectively a DSPy-team paper extending their own framework's optimizer suite. Submitted 6 Aug 2025 (arXiv v1); code released as part of the actively maintained `stanfordnlp/dspy` repository.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **LM-program formalization.** A program $\Phi(x)$ samples $(y, \rho) \sim \Phi(x)$, where $\rho = [\zeta_1, \ldots, \zeta_{|\rho|}]$ is the ordered trajectory of module calls and each trace $\zeta_t = \langle M_t, q_t, o_t \rangle$ records the invoked module, its materialized prompt, and its output — logging only LM-level calls, not other control logic (Eq. 3).
2. **Module-level grouping instead of prompt-level grouping.** Standard GRPO groups completions sharing one input prompt (Eq. 1-2); mmGRPO instead samples a meta-group of full program trajectories from a shared program-level input $x$, then aligns module calls *across* trajectories by module identity and relative order of appearance within the trajectory, forming per-module GRPO groups $\{(q_i, o_i, r_i)\}_{i=1}^G$ where members can have different prompts $q_i$ (reflecting different upstream context) but are all attributed to the same module.
3. **Uniform program-level credit assignment.** Every module-level group's reward $r_i$ is set to the same scalar program-level outcome reward $r = \mu(y, \rho, m)$ for the trajectory that produced it — the paper's deliberately simplest possible design choice, explicitly motivated by wanting a strong, modular-with-respect-to-existing-GRPO-implementations baseline rather than a more elaborate per-module credit scheme.
4. **Per-module policy update.** The modified GRPO loss (Eq. 4) is applied independently to each module-level group, updating only that module's own LM weights $\theta_M$ (in practice the same underlying LM is often shared across modules, so the update is still applied per-group even when the parameters are tied) — differing from standard GRPO in updating a per-module rather than a single shared policy, and in allowing within-group prompts to vary rather than requiring an identical shared prompt.
5. **Robustness to divergent trajectory structure.** Because control flow may branch or terminate early (e.g., a module-level parsing failure), not all trajectories from the same input $x$ have the same length or module sequence; mmGRPO optionally pads smaller groups to a fixed size before applying the loss (detailed in Appendix A) rather than requiring structurally identical rollouts.
6. **Teacher-augmented sampling.** mmGRPO can sample trajectories not only from the student program under training but also from a list of fixed teacher programs, enabling warm-starting from a prompt-optimized program or learning from a more capable LM's rollouts; on single-module programs with no teachers, mmGRPO reduces exactly to standard GRPO.
7. **BetterTogether(PO, mmGRPO) staging.** Rather than inventing a new joint objective, the paper extends BetterTogether's offline-RL recipe (first optimize prompts with MIPROv2, then weight-tune the resulting program) directly to the online setting: MIPROv2 first produces a prompt-optimized program, and mmGRPO then fine-tunes its weights, keeping the optimized prompts fixed during RL.

# Q6 Experiment Design

**LM programs and tasks.** Banking77 (single Chain-of-Thought module, 77-class intent classification, 250 train / 500 dev examples, exact-match evaluation — mmGRPO reduces to standard GRPO here since there is only one module). PAPILLON on the PUPA benchmark (two-module pipeline: redact a private query, send the redacted version to an untrusted-but-more-powerful external LM `gpt-4.1-mini`, generate the final response from its answer; composite privacy-leakage-plus-quality metric judged by a large LM; 111 train / 221 dev examples). HoVer$_{4\text{-HOP}}$ (query-generation and fact-summarization modules iterated over 4 hops with a ColBERTv2 retriever over Wikipedia snippets from the HotPotQA dump; Recall@100 over up to 100 returned passages; 500/500 train/dev examples built from the original HoVer train split, deduplicated by source HotPotQA question). Two open LMs: llama3.1-8b-instruct and qwen3-8b, run via vLLM (max context 32,768 tokens, max output tokens 1,032, up to 3 retries per module on parsing errors).

**Methods compared.** Vanilla Chain-of-Thought (untuned baseline); MIPROv2 prompt optimization alone (`auto=medium`, 12 trials, 12 few-shot and 6 instruction candidates, 80% train-set-derived validation split); mmGRPO alone (HuggingFace GRPOTrainer, 750 steps, 4 examples/step, 12 rollouts/example, LoRA rank 16/alpha 64/dropout 0.05 on projection modules [q,k,v,o,up,down,gate], learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, train context 8,192 tokens, $\beta{=}0.01$/grad-clip 0.1 for qwen3-8b and $\beta{=}0.04$/grad-clip 0.5 for llama3.1); BetterTogether(PO, mmGRPO) (MIPROv2 first, then mmGRPO on the resulting fixed-prompt program). All results are dev-set accuracy averaged over 3 seeds; the dev set plays no role in optimization.

**Main results (avg across all six LM-task pairs).** Vanilla CoT: 66.3. MIPROv2 (PO) alone: 70.0 (+5% relative to CoT). mmGRPO alone: 71.2 (+7% relative to CoT). BetterTogether(PO, mmGRPO): 73.4 — the best result in the majority of individual task-model pairs, improving 11% over Vanilla CoT, 5% over MIPROv2 alone, and 3% over mmGRPO alone. Per-task-model detail: mmGRPO alone beats MIPROv2 on Banking77-llama3.1 (63.7 vs. 59.4), roughly matches or trails it on qwen3.1 (64.9 vs. 65.9), beats MIPROv2 on HoVer-qwen3 (71.0 vs. 69.3) but trails it on HoVer-llama3.1 (60.2 vs. 63.4) and ties/slightly trails on PAPILLON-qwen3 (83.3 vs. 78.1, actually ahead) — i.e., mmGRPO does not consistently dominate MIPROv2 task-by-task even though it wins on average.

**Compute trade-off (explicitly reported as a finding, not a limitation aside).** Vanilla mmGRPO training averaged 18.7 GPU-hours on 2 H100s per run; MIPROv2 averaged only 1.4 GPU-hours on a single H100 — roughly a 13x compute-cost difference for a smaller average accuracy gain (5% vs. 7%), leading the paper to state PO is "likely much more feasible for settings which have lower computation budgets."

# Q7 Datasets and Code

All three evaluation datasets are pre-existing public benchmarks, not new releases of this paper: Banking77 (Casanueva et al. 2020), PUPA/PAPILLON (Siyan et al. 2024), HoVer built on HotPotQA (Jiang et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2018). The paper's own contribution is the mmGRPO optimizer implementation, open-sourced as `dspy.GRPO` inside the actively maintained DSPy library at [github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy) (37,989 GitHub stars per HF metadata at the time of this note — reflecting DSPy's overall popularity as a framework, not specifically this optimizer's usage) and documented at [dspy.ai](https://dspy.ai). Training uses DSPy's RL library Arbor (Ziems et al. 2025, a different "Arbor" from this wiki's own [Arbor](2606.11926.md) hypothesis-tree paper — same name, unrelated system), itself inspired by the Verifiers library. LMs used are pre-existing open-weight models (llama3.1-8b-instruct, qwen3-8b) plus a proprietary external model for PAPILLON (`gpt-4.1-mini`); no new model weights are released.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

This note is built from the full paper text (Title through Conclusion, Limitations, Acknowledgments, and the reference list) fetched via `hf papers read`; the Appendices (A: implementation/algorithm pseudocode and padding details, B: per-task LM programs and example trajectories, C: license information) were not read in full and are not the basis of any claim above beyond what the main text itself states about their existence. This is an academic multi-institution paper (not a single vendor's product report), with DSPy's own creators among the authors — a relevant conflict-of-interest note given the optimizer is released inside their own framework, though the empirical comparison (PO vs. RL vs. combined) does not obviously favor DSPy's pre-existing MIPROv2 optimizer over the paper's own new mmGRPO contribution. Results are averaged over 3 seeds, which is a modest but explicit robustness measure; no variance or confidence intervals are reported alongside the averaged numbers in the main table, so the practical significance of small per-cell differences (e.g., PAPILLON-llama3.1 MIPROv2 83.9 vs. mmGRPO 83.9, a tie) cannot be assessed from the reported table alone. The paper's own Limitations section is unusually direct about scope: only 8B-parameter models were tested (no claim about larger-model behavior), only LoRA fine-tuning was used (not compared against full-parameter updates), only one mmGRPO design (module-level uniform-credit grouping) was evaluated despite "many possible alternative formulations," and the Banking77 result is explicitly flagged as a case where reward-only training does not yet match what supervised fine-tuning of an encoder model could achieve on the same task — the paper states this outcome plainly rather than omitting or downplaying the negative result.

# Q9 Contributions

1. MMGRPO: the first GRPO implementation applicable to modular, multi-module LM programs (not just single-stage/single-turn LM calls), via module-level rollout grouping that aligns module calls across trajectories by identity and relative order rather than requiring a shared prompt or identical trajectory structure.
2. A mechanism for handling variable-length and structurally divergent trajectories (branching control flow, early termination from parsing failures) through group padding, plus support for sampling from fixed teacher programs alongside the student, letting mmGRPO warm-start from prompt-optimized programs or more capable LMs.
3. The first controlled empirical comparison of prompt optimization (MIPROv2), online multi-module RL (mmGRPO), and their staged composition (BetterTogether(PO, mmGRPO)) on the same LM programs and LMs, showing each of the three consistently improves over untuned Chain-of-Thought (5%, 7%, and 11% average relative gains respectively) and that the staged combination outperforms either component alone in the majority of task-model pairs.
4. An explicit compute-cost accounting showing mmGRPO's larger average gain over MIPROv2 (7% vs. 5%) comes at roughly 13x the GPU-hours (18.7 vs. 1.4 hours), providing a concrete resource-budget consideration for practitioners choosing between the two optimization strategies.
5. An open-source release of mmGRPO as the `dspy.GRPO` optimizer inside DSPy, making it usable as an off-the-shelf optimizer for arbitrary compound AI systems already expressed as DSPy programs, without requiring a bespoke RL implementation per program.

# Q10 Next Steps

The paper's own stated limitations, given directly rather than implied: results may not generalize to larger models than the 8B scale tested; LoRA fine-tuning may underperform full-parameter updates that were not evaluated; only one of "many possible alternative formulations" of multi-module GRPO was tested, leaving the design space of alternative module-grouping or credit-assignment schemes unexplored; and Banking77's reward-only training setting does not yet match what supervised fine-tuning could achieve on that specific task, an open negative result the paper does not claim to resolve. The paper's own conclusion explicitly calls for future work on further integrating policy-gradient RL and prompt optimization "in both offline and online settings," treating BetterTogether(PO, mmGRPO) as a first staged composition rather than a final answer on how the two should combine.

Two points beyond the paper's own framing are worth flagging for a reader assessing this note's evidence. First, mmGRPO's uniform program-level credit assignment (every module in a trajectory receives the same scalar reward regardless of which module's behavior actually drove success or failure) is the same coarse-credit design this wiki's [Agent fine-tuning](../topics/agent-fine-tuning.md) topic already tracks in Agent Lightning's per-transition and VibeWorlding's per-trajectory rewards — mmGRPO does not attempt within-trajectory credit differentiation across modules, so a module that behaved well inside an otherwise-failed trajectory is penalized identically to one that caused the failure; the paper does not report whether this coarseness limits gains specifically on the two-module programs (PAPILLON, HoVer) relative to the single-module Banking77 case, where the distinction is moot. Second, because the same underlying LM weights are shared across modules in this paper's actual experiments ("we use the same underlying LM weights for each module for lightweight training and deployment"), the reported results do not directly test the harder case mmGRPO's own formulation is designed for — independently-updated per-module weight sets — leaving open whether module-level grouping's benefit (versus simply treating the whole trajectory as one GRPO group with a shared policy) would hold up, shrink, or grow once modules genuinely diverge in their learned parameters.

* [Agent fine-tuning](../topics/agent-fine-tuning.md) - adds a distinct credit-assignment answer to this topic's running GRPO-extension comparisons: rather than Agent Lightning's per-transition decomposition or ContextPilot's per-snapshot branch-and-average estimator, mmGRPO groups at the *module* level — aligning calls to the same named module across different trajectories rather than decomposing one trajectory into sequential pieces — and is the first of this topic's papers to target genuinely heterogeneous, differently-parameterized LM components (distinct prompt templates and, in principle, distinct weight sets) rather than one policy acting repeatedly across turns.
* [GRPO](../concepts/grpo.md) - a fourth wiki instance extending GRPO beyond its single-policy, single-prompt original form (after Agent Lightning's per-transition grouping, ContextPilot's per-snapshot grouping, and WideSeek-R1's multi-agent token/agent-level reweighting), and the first to relax the "shared prompt within a group" assumption itself by grouping on module identity instead, showing GRPO's core grouped-advantage machinery survives even when group members no longer share an input.
* [DSPy](../concepts/dspy.md) - mmGRPO is released as this framework's `dspy.GRPO` optimizer, joining DSPy's pre-existing prompt-optimization optimizers (including MIPROv2, used here as both a baseline and the first stage of BetterTogether) as a second, RL-based lever for compiling an LM program without hand-writing prompts or reward-model-specific training code.
