# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** sampled gameplay video frames from AAA titles, optionally with natural-language instructions at one or more temporal horizons (short-horizon operation L₁, medium-horizon goal L₂, long-horizon strategy L₃). In the online track, the input is a live Minecraft environment plus a long-horizon task.
* **Output (Y):** offline, a choice among four options: the correct keyboard-mouse action sequence (T₁), the correct ordered sequence of L₁ operations that realizes an L₂ goal (T₂), or the consistent L₃/L₂/L₁/action bundle (T₃). Online, executed actions that complete verifiable subtasks.
* **Core problem:** there is no common, cheap, reproducible yardstick that measures both families of game-playing models on the same footing. Dedicated game agents (VLA or action-head models) act at high frequency but cannot plan. General VLMs plan well but have never been systematically measured on *action*, and their game results (e.g. Pokémon runs) depend on bespoke harnesses. Existing data covers one game or mini-games, lacks dense language instructions, and existing benchmarks rely on a handful of high-variance online rollouts that collapse distinct failures into one success rate.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Not a new problem. Game benchmarks (MineDojo, MCU, BALROG, VideoGameBench, Orak, GameWorld, GameVerse) and gameplay datasets (VPT, MineRL, NitroGen, Open-P2P, D2E) already exist. What is new is the combination the paper tabulates in its Tables 2 and 3: large-scale AAA-focused data with *directly recorded* human actions (not inverse-dynamics or gamepad-overlay pseudo-labels), dense instructions at three horizons, and an evaluation that is harness-independent and reproducible offline yet checked against online play. It matters for agent research because game playing isolates the planning-versus-acting split that also shows up in GUI and computer-use agents, under a controllable action space.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If gameplay is annotated as a nested pyramid of operations, goals, and strategies aligned to real human keyboard-mouse traces, then (a) standardized multiple-choice questions derived from it can rank heterogeneous model families in a way that agrees with actual online play, and (b) giving models medium- and long-horizon instructions should help *future-action planning*, whereas short-horizon instructions mainly help recognizing the current action.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Gameplay datasets.** Single-game: MineRL, MineDojo, VPT, STEVE-1, PLAICraft (Minecraft), WildWorld (Monster Hunter Wilds), EgoCS-400K (Counter-Strike). Multi-game: NitroGen (gamepad-overlay actions, no instructions), Open-P2P (mostly non-AAA, sparse instructions), D2E (mostly IDM-inferred actions), gaming-500-hours. Game-TARS uses costly manual instruction labels and is unreleased.

* **Game-playing models.** Dedicated agents (NitroGen, Open-P2P, JARVIS-VLA, Game-TARS, Lumine) vs. general models with bespoke harnesses (Cradle on GPT-4V, Gemini and Claude on Pokémon, SIMA 2 on Gemini).

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

Annotate bottom-up, evaluate top-down.

* **GameHorizon-Annotator.**
  1. *Action-aware segmentation* uses keyboard-mouse traces rather than visual scene cuts. Raw inputs are mapped to game-specific action semantics via keybinds, sustained actions are grouped, and discrete events (jump, attack) set boundaries. A VLM (Gemini 3.5 Flash) disambiguates inputs whose meaning is context-dependent (a left click as attack vs. item selection).
  2. A VLM writes an action-grounded L₁ instruction per short clip.
  3. Adjacent clips are merged into L₂ and then L₃ clips when the VLM judges them to serve the same goal or strategy, with dynamic programming enforcing 1-5 s, 1-2 min, and 5-8 min duration ranges.
  4. Higher-level instructions are written from frames plus constituent lower-level instructions. Action traces are included for L₁ and L₂ but omitted for L₃, to keep strategies free of operational detail.
* **Why bottom-up for data but top-down for tests.** The authors' own variant results show abstraction (L₁ → L₂) is near-ceiling (98.4%), so it is reliable for labeling. Decomposition (L₂ → L₁) is underdetermined (69.5%), so it makes a hard test.
* **GameHorizon-Bench.** The offline track has T₁ single-horizon action, T₂ multi-horizon decomposition, and T₃ cross-horizon consistency, plus ten diagnostic variants. The online track uses causal (fixed-order) and thematic (any-order) Minecraft tasks. After any failed subtask the game is reset to that subtask's success state, so a failure is localized to one step instead of voiding the run.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Offline:** 47 models — 35 general VLMs, 4 unified multimodal models (UMMs), 2 GUI agents, 3 coding agents (Claude Sonnet 5, Opus 4.8, Fable 5), and 3 dedicated game agents. T₁-T₃ have 1,000 four-option questions each and each of ten variants has 200, for 5,000 in total. Question-answering models get the same number of frames and answer directly. Game agents, which cannot answer questions, are scored on T₁ only by aligning their predicted action sequence to the options with Needleman-Wunsch; NitroGen's gamepad actions are mapped to keyboard-mouse via keybinds. Models are split into four tiers by rank.
* **Variants:** run on only four models, 200 questions each. They compare current-action perception vs. future-action planning under three input settings, top-down decomposition vs. bottom-up abstraction, and instruction-only vs. instruction-plus-action options.
* **Thinking ablation:** thinking on vs. off for a few models.
* **Online:** 12 models (three per offline tier) on 10 causal and 10 thematic Minecraft tasks totalling 62 subtasks, with 400 model calls per subtask and outcomes read from game state. A long-horizon task counts as passed only if completed end-to-end with no resets.
* **Why Minecraft online:** most AAA titles do not expose game state, so online verification was not possible on the offline games.

# Q7 Datasets and Code

GameHorizon-Data covers 5,000 hours (4,341 hours, 86.8%, kept for instruction annotation after filtering), 4,571 videos at 60 fps and 2K resolution, 21 titles, and 100 expert players. It holds 411.03 million keyboard-mouse events (mouse sampled at 20 Hz, about 2.84 keyboard events per second) and 6,184,036 instructions: 5,947,588 L₁, 189,158 L₂, and 47,290 L₃, with mean spans of 2.63 s, 82.6 s, and 330.4 s. The largest title is Valorant (617.5 h); the smallest is Honor of Kings: World (35.9 h). The paper says the dataset, annotator, and benchmark *will be* released, at [TencentARC/GameHorizon](https://github.com/TencentARC/GameHorizon) and the [project page](https://gamehorizon-suite.github.io/). Release status and licensing of recordings of commercial games are not stated in the retrieved text.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported:**
  * *Discriminability.* Question-answering models span 44.6-80.2% (mean 64.7% vs. 25% chance), with task means T₁ 57.3%, T₂ 65.1%, T₃ 71.6%.
  * *Offline-online agreement.* Offline tiers 1-4 occupy online ranks 1-3, 4-6, 7-9, and 10-12 exactly.
  * *Long-horizon difficulty.* The best online model (GPT-6-Astra) passes 45.0% of long-horizon tasks, and ten of twelve models pass at most 10.0%.
  * *Instruction horizon matters.* Short-horizon instructions raise current-action perception by 14.1 points (44.5 → 58.6) but future planning by only 0.2. Multi-horizon instructions raise future planning by a further 7.0 points (34.8 → 41.8), which is the 7.2-point gain over frames-only input quoted in the introduction.
  * *Dedicated game agents do not transfer.* JARVIS-VLA gets 54.2% on in-domain Minecraft (VPT) but 28.1% on unseen AAA games; NitroGen and Open-P2P are also near chance.
* **Weaker than the headline suggests:**
  * *Offline vs. online games differ.* The online track is Minecraft only, while the offline track is AAA titles. So "offline predicts online" is shown across games, on 12 models, as a rank ordering rather than a correlation coefficient.
  * *Annotation and evaluation share a model family.* All instructions — the answer keys and distractor material for T₂/T₃ — are VLM-generated (Gemini 3.5 Flash), and Gemini-family models rank near the top. The retrieved text reports no human validation rate for the instructions (it may be in the appendix, which this reading only partly covered).
  * *Multiple choice is not play.* It measures recognition among four options, not generation of executable control at frame rate. The authors acknowledge the reasoning-vs-latency trade-off (thinking helps strong models by 5.6-9.3 points and hurts small ones by 5.9-8.2) but do not measure latency.
  * *Small diagnostics.* Variant findings rest on four models and 200 questions per variant, with no uncertainty estimates in the retrieved text.

# Q9 Contributions

* An AAA gameplay corpus that, per the authors' comparison table, is the first to combine large scale, directly recorded human actions, and dense three-horizon instructions (release announced, not yet confirmed).
* A scalable, action-grounded bottom-up annotation pipeline that turns raw play into an instruction pyramid without manual labeling.
* A harness-independent offline benchmark that puts VLMs, UMMs, GUI agents, coding agents, and game-specific VLAs on one scale, plus a reset-based online protocol that localizes long-horizon failures to individual steps.
* Findings worth carrying forward: planning future actions is a larger bottleneck than decoding actions (T₃ drops only 74.0 → 73.4 when action sequences are added to the options). GUI-agent post-training can *hurt* transfer to games (UI-TARS-1.5-7B trails its Qwen2.5-VL-7B base by 3.7 points). Coding agents transfer surprisingly well (Claude Fable 5 at 71.2%, Tier 2).

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Online AAA evaluation:** the stepwise-reset protocol needs game-state access. Extending it beyond Minecraft (modding APIs, emulator snapshots) would test whether the offline-online agreement holds on the same games.
* **Human validation of the pyramid:** report agreement between VLM-written instructions and human annotators, and re-rank models with a non-Gemini annotator to rule out family bias.
* **Generative action scoring:** score models on producing action sequences, not only choosing among them. The paper already does this for game agents via sequence alignment, and could extend it to VLMs.
* **Training use:** the paper evaluates only. Fine-tuning a VLA or VLM on GameHorizon-Data would test whether multi-horizon instructions close the planning gap they expose.
* **Link to GUI agents (reader-constructed):** the planning-vs-perception gap and the finding that GUI post-training hurts game transfer suggest checking whether [GUI and computer-use agents](../topics/gui-computer-use-agents.md) benchmarks under-measure future-action planning in the same way.
