description: Shows that RL red-teamers with higher attack success (PISmith over RL-Hammer) can yield less robust agents after adversarial training because they repeat a few attack strategies, then proposes a two-stage fix. # Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a base tool-calling agent policy π_θ0 with white-box weights, and a sandbox of simulated task pairs (a benign user task, an injection task, and a tool environment) in which the defender knows exactly which injection string was planted.
* **Output (Y):** a hardened policy π_θ, with no added inference-time components, that under indirect prompt injection (IPI) does not execute the injection task (low attack success rate, ASR), still completes the user task (utility under attack, UA), and keeps clean-input performance (benign utility, BU).
* **Constraints:** the attacker controls only tool observations, knows the defense, and may query the deployed agent repeatedly; evaluation is gradient-free (static strings and iterative search). Training tasks, tools, and attacks are disjoint from the evaluation benchmarks.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Pairing automated red-teaming with adversarial training is established practice, and RL red-teamers (RL-Hammer, PISmith) and trained defenses (StruQ, SecAlign, Meta-SecAlign, SecOPD, RL adversarial training) all exist. The paper's new question is whether the first stage's success metric predicts the second stage's result. It reports that it does not, attributes the gap to strategy-level mode collapse that surface diversity rewards (BLEU, embedding distance, token entropy) do not prevent, and treats the two stages as one objective. The utility loss of adversarial training is a known trade-off; the token-level remedy for the IPI case is the new part.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If the red-teaming policy is rewarded for successful injections that previously discovered attack strategies cannot explain, the collected training set covers more distinct strategies, and adversarial training on it gives lower attack success on unseen attacks than training on a larger set from an ASR-maximizing red-teamer. Separately, if the defender is credited token by token for agreeing with its own "ignore the injection" behavior and not just with a trajectory outcome, it resists injections without learning to refuse or abort.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **RL red-teaming for prompt injection.** RL-Hammer (BLEU or embedding-distance diversity rewards), PISmith (adaptive entropy bonus, dynamic advantage weighting), both on GRPO.
* **Training-based IPI defenses.** StruQ (SFT), SecAlign and Meta-SecAlign (preference optimization), SecOPD (on-policy distillation from a clean-context teacher), RL adversarial training, Instruction Hierarchy.
* **Non-training defenses.** Spotlighting, Sandwiching, PromptGuard, PIGuard, DataSentinel, MELON, CaMeL.
* **Attacks.** Static: IgnorePrev, InjecAgent, ImportantMsgs, ToolKnow, ChatInject. Iterative-adaptive: TAP, Genetic (MAP-Elites), AutoInject.
* **Borrowed techniques.** Strategy decomposition and sparse dictionary learning from jailbreak research; on-policy self-distillation (OPSD); per-reward advantage normalization.
* **Key people.** Lipeng He, Yihan Wang (corresponding), and N. Asokan (University of Waterloo; Asokan also KTH), Jiawen Zhang (Zhejiang University). Likely critics: authors of adaptive-attack evaluations, who would ask for larger attack budgets and stronger attacker models than a 4B LLM.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Strategy primitives.** An LLM extractor turns each successful injection into records (strategy name, matched text, task-agnostic explanation). Explanation embeddings are factored by sparse dictionary learning into K shared primitives; each primitive is named from its top-weighted records and near-duplicates are merged. Two examples in the paper: "signal-based instruction override" (pretend the task is finished, then reset instructions) and "task-framing instruction" (present the injected goal as workflow metadata).
2. **Explainability Score (ES).** A new injection's explanation is sparse-coded against the dictionary, and an LLM grader rates from 1 to 5 how completely the top primitives explain it; rescaled to [0, 1]. ES near 1 means a rephrasing of known strategies.
3. **Pipette-Red.** The red-teamer (initialized from the base policy, trained with GRPO against the frozen base policy) receives a binary attack reward, a format reward, and a coverage reward r_cov = success × (1 − ES). The dictionary is rebuilt every M steps from retained records plus new ones with ES below a threshold, so repeating either an old or a newly found strategy stops paying. Each reward is normalized within the group before weighting.
4. **Pipette-Blue.** After an SFT warm-up on self-generated demonstrations, the agent is trained on-policy to reason before each tool call through two checks: C1, does the last observation contain an instruction or redirection attempt; C2, does every planned call serve a goal the user authorized. Rewards: the outcome reward (user task done and injection task not done), an LLM-judged reasoning reward, and a token-level term r_tok = log π(y | context, z⁺) − log π(y | context, z⁻), where z⁺ is a privileged prefix telling the model to ignore the known injection and z⁻ one telling it to follow it. The matched pair cancels the formatting effect of adding a prefix.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Training.** 560 AgentDyn task pairs for both stages. Red-teaming baselines run 250 steps at their authors' batch sizes.
* **Models.** Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, GPT-OSS-20B, Qwen3.6-27B.
* **Evaluation.** AgentDojo (Workspace, Slack, Travel, Banking) and Agent Security Bench (ASB), with no shared user or injection tasks. Static attacks on 420 AgentDojo pairs per attack and BU on 97 clean tasks; iterative-adaptive TAP (up to 250 attempts per pair) and Genetic (up to 248) on 40 AgentDojo pairs, 10 per suite, with Qwen3.5-4B as attacker and Qwen3-4B as judge.
* **Comparisons.** Stage-wise: each red-teamer's dataset × {RL, SFT} adversarial training; then RL, SFT, and Pipette-Blue on the Pipette-Red dataset. Full system against Meta-SecAlign (Llama-8B), SecOPD (Qwen3.6-27B), native Instruction Hierarchy (GPT-OSS-20B), and seven non-training defenses on the two small models. Ablation of the reasoning checks and r_tok on Qwen3-4B.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

**Motivating study.** PISmith compromises more training pairs than RL-Hammer (68.75% against 33.39% on Qwen3-4B; 27.32% against 25.18% on Llama-8B) and collects more strings (7,132 against 4,282; 1,783 against 1,671), yet gives a higher worst-case ASR over ChatInject, TAP, and Genetic in three of four settings: 47.5% against 27.5% (Qwen3-4B, RL), 27.5% against 25.0% (Llama-8B, RL), 20.0% against 15.0% (Llama-8B, SFT); the exception is Qwen3-4B SFT (12.5% against 15.0%). Dictionaries built from either baseline plateau in how well they explain unseen ChatInject attacks (ES about 0.20 and 0.35 for RL-Hammer), while Pipette-Red reaches about 0.60 and 0.77 (five seeds).

**Pipette-Red.** Highest train-time ASR (70.54% with 8,299 strings on Qwen3-4B; 41.07% with 2,112 on Llama-8B) and the lowest worst-case ASR in all four settings: 20.0% (RL) and 2.5% (SFT) on Qwen3-4B, 10.0% and 5.0% on Llama-8B.

**Static attacks.** Average ASR over five AgentDojo attacks is 0.48% (Qwen3-4B) and 0.43% (Llama-8B), with average UA 57.86% and 39.05%, above every non-training defense and above the undefended model; on ASB, 0.70% and 0.50%. PromptGuard and CaMeL beat it on ChatInject ASR alone, at much lower UA (16.19% and 33.81% against 58.10% on Qwen3-4B). BU on the larger models: 92.78% against 93.81% undefended on Qwen3.6-27B (SecOPD 84.54%), 49.48% against 50.52% on GPT-OSS-20B.

**Ablation (Qwen3-4B, ChatInject).** Outcome reward only: ASR 3.57%, UA 45.48%, BU 59.79%. Adding the reasoning checks: 2.38%, 56.90%, 60.82%. Adding r_tok: 1.43%, 58.10%, 64.95%.

**Limits on the evidence.**

* The adaptive results are on 40 pairs, so one pair is 2.5 points and "0% to 10%" is 0 to 4 successful pairs; every Table V ASR is a multiple of 2.5 (reader arithmetic). No confidence intervals or seeds are reported for the trained defenders.
* The attacker and judge LLMs are 4B models with about 250 attempts; the paper states a larger budget may reach higher ASR.
* The red-teamers differ in dataset size and sampling as well as strategy coverage, so the stage-one comparison does not isolate coverage; the paper says it does not establish low coverage as the sole cause. The ES grader has not been validated against human labels (both stated).
* ES coverage is measured against one unseen attack family, ChatInject, which is also one of the three attacks in the worst-case ASR metric.
* The motivating reversal is 3 of 4 settings, two of them by one or two pairs (27.5% against 25.0%, 20.0% against 15.0%) (reader arithmetic).
* Pipette is a single red-then-blue pass; vulnerabilities that appear only in the hardened model are untested (stated). Baseline trained defenses are other groups' checkpoints trained on different data, so those comparisons mix method and training-set effects (reader observation).
* On Qwen3.6-27B, UA under adaptive attack drops from 97.50% to 87.50% and 90.00%.

# Q9 Contributions

1. Evidence that train-time attack success and dataset size from RL red-teaming do not predict post-training robustness, with a strategy-level coverage measure (ES) that shows the baselines plateau.
2. A coverage reward built on a rolling strategy dictionary that keeps the red-teamer exploring without retraining the defender.
3. An on-policy defense that combines task-alignment reasoning with a contrastive privileged-prefix token reward and keeps benign utility at or above the base model on two small models.
4. Cross-benchmark evaluation (train on AgentDyn, test on AgentDojo and ASB) against training-based and non-training defenses, including adaptive search.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Stated by the paper.** Multi-round purple-teaming that red-teams the hardened policy; larger adaptive budgets; human validation of the ES grader.
* **Isolate coverage.** Subsample the three red-teamers' datasets to equal size and equal per-task counts, then repeat adversarial training, to test whether coverage alone explains the robustness gap (reader suggestion).
* **Stronger attackers.** Rerun TAP and Genetic with a frontier attacker model and with a Pipette-aware attacker that targets the C1/C2 reasoning format directly (reader suggestion).
* **Composition with system-level defenses.** A trained policy and an egress or control-flow layer fail differently; measuring them together on the same pairs would show whether the residual 0 to 4 adaptive successes are the cases a target-admission rule would block (reader suggestion).
