title: "Mosaic: Multi-Session Agentic Reinforcement Learning"
description: Trains agents whose trajectories span several sessions, created by context compaction or subagent forking, instead of one continuous context. It replaces broadcasting one trajectory advantage to every session with a critic-free hierarchy. The trajectory advantage is rescaled by the within-outcome rank of session count, favoring fewer sessions on successes and more on failures without changing its sign. Each non-final session then gets a shaping term from the change it causes in the policy's log-likelihood of the ground-truth answer, normalized per session type. On BrowseComp with Qwen3.5-4B, compaction-mode accuracy rises from 35.31 (broadcasting) to 38.86 with 2.35 instead of 3.17 sessions; the subagent-mode gain is about one point.

# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a deep-search question x with a reference answer y*, and a policy π_θ (Qwen3.5-4B) running a ReAct-style loop with two tools, `search` (Serper) and `visit` (Jina Reader). A rollout is a set of N_i *sessions*, each an ordinary multi-turn segment with its own context. New sessions arise in two ways: *compaction*, where the agent compresses its history into a shorter prefix when the context nears its limit (each session is capped at 128K tokens), and *subagent forking*, where the main agent delegates a subtask to a non-nested subagent through a dedicated action.
* **Output (Y):** an updated policy, trained from per-session advantages that replace the single trajectory advantage. Every session is a separate training segment, and the token-level losses of all sessions in a rollout are aggregated into one token-weighted trajectory objective, so each rollout keeps equal weight within its group whatever its session count.
* **Reward:** a binary outcome R ∈ {0, 1} judged by an LLM (deepseek-v4-flash) against the reference answer.
* **Constraints:** critic-free, group-relative optimization built on GRPO, with no learned value model; the session-level signal needs a reference answer.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

New as a stated setting. Most agentic RL methods train on single-session trajectories, one continuously accumulated context per rollout, while agents at inference increasingly compact their context or fork subagents, which breaks that assumption and creates a train-inference mismatch. The common workaround, *broadcasting*, copies the trajectory-level outcome advantage to every token of every session (the paper cites Accio-Lab's Dressage and POLAR). It makes multi-session rollouts trainable, but it cannot tell a useful session from a wasted one and gives no control over how many sessions a trajectory uses. The paper names the general setting *Multi-Session Agentic RL* (any number and type of sessions per trajectory) and proposes a method for it. Neighbors are CompactionRL, which jointly optimizes task execution and context summarization, and IGPO, which uses information gain as a turn-level reward in single-session search agents.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If the trajectory advantage is adjusted by how many sessions a trajectory used, in a direction that depends on whether it succeeded, and if each non-final session receives a local reward equal to how much it raised the policy's likelihood of the correct answer, then multi-session RL will reach higher accuracy than broadcasting while using fewer sessions. A further claim from the analysis: effective multi-session optimization works by raising the utility of each session and allocating sessions better, not by using more of them.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

Mosaic optimizes at two levels and adds them.

**Session-aware trajectory advantage (Section 3.1).** Three ordering constraints define what session count should do. C1: among successes, fewer sessions should earn a higher advantage (less execution cost). C2: among failures, more sessions should earn a higher, still negative, advantage, since giving up early may mean too little exploration. C3: the outcome must dominate, so no failure can outrank a success. To satisfy them, the group is split by outcome, and each trajectory's session count is turned into a centered rank u_i ∈ [−1, 1] within its outcome subgroup (ranks rather than raw counts, to bound the signal against long-tailed counts). The GRPO advantage is then multiplied by (1 − ρ u_i) with ρ = 0.2. Because the factor stays between 1 − ρ and 1 + ρ, it can shrink or grow the advantage but never flip its sign; Appendix A proves C1-C3 for mixed-outcome groups and explains why a multiplicative rule was chosen over an additive one that could reverse the outcome's direction. When all rollouts in a group share an outcome, the GRPO advantage is zero, so a fallback supplies a small signal: −β₁u_i for all-success groups (β₁ = 0.2) and β₀ min(u_i, 0) for all-failure groups (β₀ = 0.1), so that no failure ever gets a positive advantage.

**Session-level shaping from information gain (Section 3.2).** Each session is a transition from one context state to another, and there are three types: *normal* (reasoning and tool calls), *compaction* (full history in, compressed prefix out), and *subagent* (the main agent's context before delegation, and after the result is merged back). The *potential* of a state is the pre-update policy's mean log-probability of the reference answer's tokens, computed by teacher forcing after a fixed continuation ("Now there's enough information to answer." followed by the answer tags). A session's reward is the change in potential across it, with gradients stopped. Rewards are normalized within the prompt group *separately for each session type*, because compaction sessions have a lower mean gain than normal ones and would otherwise be pushed down systematically. The last session gets zero, since its output contains the final answer, whose likelihood would swamp the signal. The session advantage is the trajectory advantage plus λ_r = 0.1 times the normalized reward. Two gates keep the local term from overriding the outcome: in all-failure groups whose base advantage is near zero, positive shaping is removed, and any shaping term that would reverse the sign of a nonzero base advantage is dropped (Appendix B.2).

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Benchmark.** [BrowseComp](../concepts/browsecomp.md), 1,266 questions, chosen because its difficulty demands long interactions that produce multi-session trajectories.
* **Training.** REDSearcher_RL_1k (1,000 deep-search tasks), one epoch, rollout batch size 16, group size 8, colocated with alternating rollout and training phases to keep asynchrony out of the comparison. Hyperparameters ρ = 0.2, β₀ = 0.1, β₁ = 0.2, λ_r = 0.1.
* **Baselines.** The base model; GRPO trained single-session and run both single-session and multi-session (test-time scaling without multi-session training); IGPO trained single-session; Broadcasting trained multi-session; Mosaic trained multi-session. All multi-session configurations are run in both the compaction and the subagent scenario, each with its own agent loop for rollouts and evaluation.
* **Analyses.** Component ablations (Figure 3c-d); reward and session-count curves during training (Figure 4); the relation between advantage and session count, and the distributions of information gain under global versus type-conditioned normalization (Figure 5); the cost of computing information gain against IGPO's turn-level version (Figure 3b); and session-level patterns: count, share of sessions with positive information gain, and information gain per session (Table 2).
* **Not included.** One base model and one benchmark; no variance across seeds; no sensitivity study of ρ, β, or λ_r; no direct comparison with CompactionRL.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * *Multi-session training beats broadcasting in the compaction scenario.* Mosaic reaches 38.86 on BrowseComp with 2.35 sessions per trajectory, against 35.31 with 3.17 for Broadcasting, a 10.1% relative gain (3.55 points) with fewer sessions (Table 1). Both beat single-session GRPO run with compaction (34.68) and the untrained base (24.80).
  * *Single-session training does not transfer well to compaction.* Single-session GRPO gains from compaction at inference (31.99 to 34.68) but uses only 1.24 sessions, while IGPO rises from 28.52 to 31.36 with 4.12 sessions under compaction and drops to 24.64 with subagents; the paper reads this as multi-session ability not emerging from generalization alone.
  * *Session counts are steered as intended.* In mixed-outcome groups, advantages fall with session count for successes and rise for failures while successes stay above failures (Figure 5). Session count grows during training for both methods but levels off with Mosaic (Figure 4).
  * *Type-conditioned normalization matters.* Under global normalization compaction sessions, whose raw gains are lower, are pushed down as a type; per-type normalization gives both types positive and negative signals (Figure 5).
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * *The subagent gain is small, and single-session GRPO nearly matches it.* In the subagent scenario Mosaic scores 32.46 against 31.44 for Broadcasting and 31.91 for single-session GRPO run with subagents, which also uses fewer sessions (2.09 against 3.77). On 1,266 questions one point is about 13 questions, and no variance is reported, so the subagent result does not separate the methods (reader observation from Table 1). The authors' ablation also finds the two components add only modestly here and suggest that marginal gain misestimates a session's contribution when trajectories form a graph rather than a chain.
  * *Session-quality metrics share the training signal.* Table 2's share of sessions with positive information gain (59.92% against 47.74% under compaction) and gain per session are measured with the same likelihood potential Mosaic rewards, so part of the rise is the optimized quantity moving (reader caveat).
  * *Narrow scope.* One 4B model, one epoch on 1,000 tasks, one benchmark, a single judge model, and fixed hyperparameters. The ablations and the information-gain cost comparison appear only in figures.
  * *The local signal needs a reference answer.* The potential is the likelihood of a ground-truth answer string, which suits short-answer QA. It does not carry over directly to tasks scored by tests or rubrics, and a compaction summary could raise it by mentioning a candidate answer rather than by keeping useful evidence (reader concern).

# Q9 Contributions

* A formulation of multi-session agentic RL: trajectories as sets of sessions trained as separate segments, broadcasting as the baseline, and a token-weighted aggregation that keeps rollouts equally weighted.
* A sign-preserving, bounded session-count modulation of the GRPO advantage with stated ordering constraints, proofs, and a fallback for groups with identical outcomes.
* Session-level shaping from answer-likelihood information gain, normalized per session type and gated so that it cannot reverse the outcome signal.
* Experiments in two multi-session scenarios (compaction and subagent forking) and an analysis of session quantity, quality, and allocation.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **From the authors.** Open training data and execution environments for multi-session RL; learning cross-session state transfer (what compaction keeps, what a fork hands over, how a merge reports back) as one objective; reliable multi-session optimization under asynchronous sampling and policy staleness, where favoring finished rollouts would bias toward short trajectories; RL inside partly opaque production harnesses such as Claude Code, including mechanisms that break context continuity (dropped reasoning tokens or tool responses).
* **Graph-aware credit for forks (reader suggestion).** Give a subagent session credit through every later session it influences, not only its immediate gain, and test whether that recovers the compaction-scenario margin in the subagent scenario.
* **A signal without reference strings (reader suggestion).** Replace the answer-likelihood potential with test outcomes or rubric scores on coding or report tasks, or with the likelihood of the agent's own final answer, and check whether type-conditioned normalization still helps.
* **Seeds and a CompactionRL comparison (reader suggestion).** Report several seeds per configuration and compare against CompactionRL, which trains the summarizer and the executor together.
