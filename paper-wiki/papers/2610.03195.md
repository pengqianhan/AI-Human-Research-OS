# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a user request from one of three domains (Shopping: 1,500 WebShop goals; Accommodation: 214 HotelQuEST requests plus 572 location-substituted variants; Scholar: 2,536 ScholarGym queries; 4,822 in total), an LLM agent in a ReAct loop with a Tavily web search tool that returns up to 10 results (title, snippet, URL; 8.83 on average), and a budget of five environment actions. The agent cannot open pages; it ends the episode by selecting any subset of the latest results.
* **Output (Y):** for each model and domain, a preference score τ_s per source (the site's registrable domain), a label (preferred "Source+", dispreferred "Source−", or neutral), inversion rates when items differ by one satisfied requirement, and the effect of interventions on the displayed source, the training data, and the prompt.
* **Core problem:** when agents choose products, hotels, or citations for users, a preference for some sources narrows what users see and can pass over better items. A raw selection rate cannot show this, because a source's items may simply satisfy the request better or sit higher in the list. The paper asks whether source preference survives those controls in end-to-end search, whether the displayed source information causes it, and how it can arise and be reduced.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. Source and authority effects on LLM choices are documented: Khan et al. (ICLR 2026) attach different sources to semantically identical content and find latent source preferences; Dai et al. (EMNLP 2025) find media-source bias in LLM citations; Schuster et al. study source preferences under knowledge conflicts; BiasBusters finds provider and position bias in tool selection; Allouah et al. and Cherep et al. study biases in shopping agents. What is new is the setting: agents retrieve their own items from the live web, so items from different sources differ in content and quality, and the paper builds a matched-comparison design to separate source from quality and position. It also links the observational result to causal interventions (hide, restore, swap the source), to a training mechanism, and to inference-time mitigations. The problem matters for users, who may receive worse items, and for providers, whose exposure an agent allocates.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

1. **Existence.** If items that satisfy exactly the same requirements are compared at the same display position, agents will still select some sources more and others less, and models will largely agree on which (Table 1, Figure 2, Table E.1).
2. **Override.** Source preference can outweigh request satisfaction, so the less satisfying item is chosen far more often when it carries the preferred source (inversion rates, Figure 3).
3. **Causation by displayed source.** Hiding URLs and source names weakens the preference, restoring them widens it, and relabeling the same content with a preferred source raises its selection (Figure 4, Table 3).
4. **Training route.** If preference training repeatedly pairs a source with the better item, the agent learns the source as a shortcut and favors it even when items are equally good; rebalancing the pairing weakens an existing preference (Tables 4-5).
5. **Inference route.** When an item lacks information the request asks for (for example a price), the agent fills the gap with preconceptions about the source; supplying the information or countering the preconception reduces the preference (Table 6).

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Preference and bias in LLM choices.** Order sensitivity in multiple-choice questions (Pezeshkpour and Hruschka; Zheng et al.), authority and social-proof cues (Mammen et al.; Filandrianos et al.), source preference in retrieval-augmented generation (Li et al., ACL 2025), latent source preferences across papers, news, and shopping (Khan et al.), consumer-choice experiments with agents (Cherep et al.), and tool-selection bias among functionally equivalent tools (BiasBusters, whose cyclic-rotation design this paper adopts).

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

The core contribution is a measurement design that isolates source from quality and position in naturally retrieved result lists, followed by interventions that test causes.

* **Requirement-matched pairs.** A source-blind LLM judge (Qwen3.8-27B, URLs and source names removed) marks which requirements each result's title and snippet satisfy. Two items from different sources in the same list with the identical satisfied set form a matched pair.
* **Position control.** Each list of length L is rerun in all L cyclic rotations (a cyclic Latin square), so each item appears once at every position. For each position the two items' outcomes form one comparison with selection difference D ∈ {−1, 0, 1}.
* **Preference score.** A Bradley-Terry model with Davidson's tie parameter, ridge penalty λ = 0.005, and rarely compared sources pooled into one opponent, gives a rating per source; τ_s is the expected selection difference against an average source (for example, τ_s = 0.10 means selected 10 points more often). A request-level cluster bootstrap (B = 10,000) and 5% FDR per model and domain assign Source+, Source−, or neutral.
* **Inversion rate.** For pairs that differ by exactly one satisfied requirement, Inv_{ℓ,m} is the share of exactly-one-selected comparisons where the less satisfying item is chosen, split by the two sources' labels.
* **Interventions.** Disclosure conditions (Hidden, URL-only, Original) replayed on frozen trajectories; a counterbalanced source swap with content held fixed; DPO with fake source names (Kelto/Varnis and two other pairs) assigned to the better item in 50%, 80%, or 20% of training pairs; Amazon rebalancing against eBay, Etsy, and AliExpress; and prompt or content interventions for a missing price.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Agents.** 12 models: GPT-5.4-nano, GPT-5.6-Luna, Gemini-3.7-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek-v4-Flash, Llama-4-Maverick, Llama-4-Scout, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Tulu-3-8B, Qwen3.5-27B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Shared system prompt, temperature 0 and seed 42 where supported (GPT-5.6-Luna requires temperature 1).
* **Judge validation.** Two human annotators plus the judge label 375 Shopping, 375 Accommodation, and 100 Scholar results at the requirement level; judge-human Krippendorff's α is 0.65-0.75 against human-human 0.67-0.72 (Table D.1).
* **Scale.** Table E.1 reports per model and domain the requests with comparisons, matched pairs, position comparisons (for example 9,857 pairs and 86,362 comparisons for GPT-5.4-nano on Shopping), and the number of rated sources.
* **Robustness.** Refitting with λ ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.1, 1} moves no source between Source+ and Source−; rank correlation with the main fit is at least 0.98; the inversion order holds for every λ in 35 of 36 cells.
* **Interventions.** Disclosure replay on 11 models (Gemini omitted for cost); swap experiment on all 12 with four evaluations per pair; DPO on Qwen3.5-9B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Tulu-3-8B-SFT with 5,000 training pairs (LoRA, 5,000 updates, β = 0.1), evaluated by comparing the log probabilities of the two selection actions on 1,000 equal-satisfaction pairs; missing-price interventions on five models in Shopping.
* **Not designed in.** Agents never open pages, so all evidence is title, snippet, and URL; requirement satisfaction is judged from the same snippet. The DPO study uses a two-item selection prompt rather than the end-to-end agent. Mitigations are tested only for Shopping and only for the price gap.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Well supported.**
  * The existence claim rests on a careful design: matched satisfaction sets, full position rotation, cluster bootstrap, FDR control, and a penalty sensitivity check. Agreement across models is strong: for 10 of the 12 most frequent sources no model prefers a source that another avoids, and for 27 of 32 labeled sources in Figure 2 every model has the same sign.
  * The override result is large and consistent: median Inv_{+,−} 68% against Inv_{−,+} 2% and Inv_{0,0} 19%, with the order holding across model-domain cells.
  * The swap experiment is the strongest causal evidence: with content and position balanced, displaying Source+ instead of Source− raises selection in every model and domain (significant at p < 0.01 in at least one domain for all 12 models; up to +64.7 points for Llama-4-Maverick in Shopping).
  * The DPO study shows the shortcut mechanism is sufficient: target-source selection stays near 50% under Balanced training and moves to 70.1-75.1% under Aligned and 24.5-28.5% under Reversed on equal-satisfaction pairs.
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * The snippet-only setting is a strong scope limit. Snippets often omit price or details, which the paper itself shows triggers source-based guessing; agents that open pages may show smaller preferences.
  * "Same requirements" means the same *judged* requirements from a snippet; judge-human agreement is only moderate (α 0.65-0.75), so some matched pairs differ in ways the judge missed, and that noise could favor sources whose snippets carry more cues.
  * The training route is shown to be possible, not shown to be the cause of the deployed models' preferences; Appendix I's correlation between a source's mean satisfaction and its τ_s is descriptive, and the authors say it "does not establish what their training data contained".
  * The mitigation results are small in scope: five models, one domain, one missing attribute. The effective prompt ("Designate") counteracts one preconception with another and requires knowing which source is dispreferred, as the paper notes.
  * Live web results and API-served models are not deterministic, so exact reruns may differ.
* **Reader observation.** The paper treats preference as unwarranted relative to requirements visible in the snippet. Some of it may be a reasonable prior about unobserved attributes (reliability of listings, price transparency). Separating rational priors from unwarranted shortcuts would need ground truth on the items' full pages, which this design does not collect.

# Q9 Contributions

* A measurement method for source preference in naturally retrieved result lists: requirement-matched pairs, cyclic position rotation, and a Bradley-Terry-Davidson score with cluster-bootstrap significance and FDR control.
* Evidence across 12 models and three domains that agents share preferences for specific sites, including among sites of the same type (Walmart preferred by all 12 models, Target by two; Booking.com by ten, Expedia avoided by half).
* A quantified harm: preferred sources win against better items from dispreferred sources about two-thirds of the time.
* Causal evidence that the displayed source itself changes selection, with most of the effect carried by the URL alone (over 80% of the gap widening in nine of 11 models).
* A demonstrated training route (source as a DPO shortcut) and two mitigations (supply missing information; counter the preconception in the system prompt).

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Browsing agents.** Repeat the study with agents that open and read pages, to see whether richer evidence shrinks the preference or whether URLs still dominate.
* **Ground-truth quality.** Collect full-page or transaction-level ground truth to separate justified source priors from shortcuts.
* **Training audits.** Measure source-satisfaction correlations in real preference and RL data for agent models, and test whether balancing them during post-training reduces end-to-end preference without hurting selection quality.
* **General mitigations.** The working prompt names the dispreferred source; a source-agnostic method (for example, masking URLs during the judgment step and restoring them only for citation) would be easier to deploy.
* **Beyond selection.** The same matched design could measure source preference in citation by deep-research agents and in tool or MCP server choice, where BiasBusters found related provider bias.
* **Project fit (reader note).** This repository's own paper-search and wiki workflows select sources on the human's behalf (arXiv, OpenReview, Medium, and others appear among the measured Scholar sources); the finding is a reason to record which source a retrieved item came from and to judge relevance before showing the source.
