# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** for model training, tasks paired with a real, tool-mediated execution environment (file systems, code interpreters, search, applications) plus an external verification signal (tests, metrics, file/application state, geometric checks, source-grounding, or human-defined criteria); for the human–AI collaboration study, 769 structured task-review records from 56 participants developing Atria Dawn itself, each recording task category, whether AI was used, feasibility without AI, who proposed vs. selected each decision (goals/scope, methods/parameters, acceptance criteria), what difficulty arose and how it was resolved, and the disposition of the AI's output — together with corresponding agent action logs.
* **Output (Y):** Atria Dawn Preview, a 744B-parameter mixture-of-experts agentic language model evaluated on 16 public agentic benchmarks (tool use, search/research, workspace productivity, professional deliverables, software/terminal/ML engineering, cybersecurity); and, from the collaboration study, quantitative role-distribution statistics (proposal share, final-decision share, difficulty-resolution mode, output-revision mode) characterizing how responsibility was actually split between humans and agents during one large real AI R&D project.
* **Core problem:** as agentic models take on more of the mechanics of AI research and engineering, task-level benchmark performance alone cannot answer who is actually driving a development project — who identifies worthwhile problems, chooses among proposed methods, interprets uncertain results, and decides what to pursue next — and that division of responsibility is what determines whether AI-assisted development is heading toward genuine recursive self-improvement (RSI) or remains bounded by human judgment at every consequential decision point.
* **Scope:** one model family (Atria Dawn Preview, built on a 744B-parameter Z.ai MoE base) evaluated on 16 named public benchmarks under the paper's own reported protocol (Appendix B), plus a single-project, single-organization case study of that same model's own development cycle (Aug 7–Sep 4, 2026 window for the agent-actions-per-prompt trend; 769 total task records; 56 participants) — not a multi-organization or multi-project comparison, and not an independent replication of the benchmark numbers.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The paper does not claim to originate agentic tool-use training, verifiable-reward-style training pipelines, or RSI as a research goal — it cites AlphaEvolve, Novikov et al.'s algorithm-discovery agents, and Lu et al.'s "towards end-to-end automation of AI research" as precedent for agents proposing, testing, and revising solutions during AI development, and situates itself explicitly alongside contemporaneous OpenAI ("Research acceleration: the view inside OpenAI") and Anthropic ("Agentic coding and persistent returns to expertise") reports describing agents taking on more AI-R&D responsibility. Its stated novelty is pairing a competitive model release with a quantitative, task-attributed account of its own development process — decomposing "AI helped" into who proposed an option vs. who selected it, and who diagnosed a difficulty vs. who executed the fix — rather than reporting only aggregate delegation counts or anecdote. This directly extends [The Last AI Built by Humans](2609.11873.md)'s autonomy-level and RSI-challenge framing with a concrete empirical instance: where that paper builds a five-level taxonomy and a cross-domain progress index from external benchmark trajectories, Atria Dawn supplies a single project's internal decision-attribution data as one grounded case study of where current practice sits on such a scale.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If training data is constructed so every task is grounded in a real, tool-mediated execution environment with an externally checked outcome (rather than static supervision or unverified self-generated feedback), the resulting model should generalize its execution, error-recovery, and tool-use behavior across many agentic domains rather than overfitting to one — predicting competitive-to-leading performance across a broad span of the 16 evaluated benchmarks rather than a narrow specialty. Separately and more centrally, the paper's development-record study tests an implicit hypothesis about the current stage of human–AI collaboration: if agents have moved from task-level execution to genuine research partnership, then agents should not only execute more of the work (a rising agent-actions-per-human-prompt ratio) but also originate a growing share of substantive decisions; the paper's own evidence supports the first claim (execution volume) but complicates the second — proposal share is domain-dependent and final-decision authority stays concentrated with humans regardless of proposal source — which the paper argues shows current AI R&D has reached project-level partnership (task execution plus option generation) without yet reaching the judgment-level autonomy sustained RSI would require.

(2026, "Towards end-to-end automation of AI research," Nature) are cited as establishing that agents can propose candidates, run experiments, and revise solutions from feedback — the capability trend this paper's Section 1 opens from before asking who directs that trend.
* **Contemporaneous industry reports on AI-assisted AI R&D.** OpenAI's "Research acceleration: the view inside OpenAI" (2026) and Anthropic's "Agentic coding and persistent returns to expertise" (Hitzig et al., 2026) are cited as parallel observations that agents are taking on more R&D responsibility elsewhere in the field; the paper positions its own 769-task decision-attribution dataset as a more granular, quantitative complement to these qualitative industry accounts. It also cites OpenAI's internal finding that "over half of successful 4–8 hour [agent] tasks involve at least one human intervention" as directly comparable to, and consistent with, its own 76.0%-human-intervention difficulty-resolution figure, while noting its own data further separates steering (informational help) from takeover (direct human edits).
* **RSI framing and autonomy taxonomies.** The paper explicitly frames its Section 3 "three stages" (research object → task runner → project-level coworker → speculative RSI) as its own taxonomy rather than citing an external one directly, but this wiki's [The Last AI Built by Humans](2609.11873.md) supplies a more formal, externally validated five-level version of the same progression (improvement-execution through recursive-inheritance autonomy) and a cross-domain Headroom-Closed Index — see [Related](#related) below for how the two connect.
* **Foundation base model.** Atria Dawn Preview is built on a 744-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts foundation model attributed to Z.ai (2026); the paper does not further disclose this base model's own training details, treating it as a fixed substrate its Verifiable Experience Pipeline trains on top of.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Verifiable Experience Pipeline.** Every training task is connected to a real execution environment: the model observes state, calls tools, produces intermediate artifacts, and adapts to feedback, with final outcomes checked against external signals (executable tests, experiment metrics, file/application state, geometric structure, source-grounding, or human-defined criteria) rather than a learned or self-reported reward.
2. **Trajectory curation and failure-driven task construction.** Curation removes incomplete, contradictory, duplicate, or behaviorally invalid trajectories while preserving the task→trajectory→artifact→verification-evidence link; recurring failure patterns (ineffective tool selection, incomplete verification, missing evidence, failed recovery) are fed back into constructing new tasks and refining environments and quality checks, including using independently-established failed runs as diagnostic training cases.
3. **Long-workflow experience capture.** For extended workflows, recorded experience includes launching jobs, inspecting logs and artifacts, and revising execution plans, aimed at teaching reusable state-inspection, feedback-interpretation, and error-recovery behavior rather than single-turn tool calls.
4. **A 4-stage taxonomy of human–AI R&D roles.** The paper frames the current moment as stage (c) of a 4-stage progression — (a) AI as research object, (b) AI as task-level runner inside human-designed workflows, (c) AI as project-level coworker that plans and iterates toward human-set goals with human judgment concentrated at higher-level decisions, (d) a speculative future RSI stage — and uses this frame to interpret its own development data.
5. **Fine-grained decision-attribution methodology.** Rather than a single "was AI used" binary, the collaboration study separately measures, per task: who proposed each option (goals/scope, methods/parameters, acceptance criteria) vs. who made the final selection; what kind of help resolved the most consequential difficulty (informational context/diagnosis vs. direct human edit/takeover vs. agent self-recovery); and who performed substantive revisions to the AI's main output — a methodology explicitly designed to distinguish "AI executes more" from "AI decides more."

# Q6 Experiment Design

**Model evaluation (Table 1, Sec. 2.2).** 16 public benchmarks grouped into General Agentic Intelligence (12 benchmarks: AutomationBench, BFCL v4, CyberGym, DeepSearchQA, Workspace-Bench-Lite, BrowseComp, SkillsBench, Workspace-Bench, WideSearch, DeepResearch Bench II, τ3-Bench Banking, GDPval, JobBench) and Agentic Coding (SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench 2.1, MLE-bench Lite, CyberGym counted in both groupings by domain). Scores are reported "as published on the official release website" against six comparison models (DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813, KIMI K3, Qwen 3.8 Max, GLM 5.3, GPT 5.6 sol, Claude Opus 5); missing entries are treated as unreported, not zero. Atria Dawn achieves the top reported score on 5 of 16 (AutomationBench 53.8, BFCL v4 77.0, CyberGym 86.5, DeepSearchQA 96.0, BrowseComp 92.5) and second place within 2 points on 3 more (SkillsBench, Workspace-Bench, Workspace-Bench-Lite). On SWE-bench Pro (59.6) and Terminal-Bench 2.1 (78.3) it trails GLM 5.3/Claude Opus 5; on GDPval (1583) and JobBench (50.3) it beats several comparison models but not the strongest (Claude Opus 5 at 1768/68.0).

**Selected case studies (Sec. 2.3).** Non-benchmark demonstrations across four release categories — Discovery (weather-forecasting ViT trained on >100GB data, 69 variables, 45k steps; Gated Delta Network decode optimization reaching a 1.46× dev-measured latency ratio via a Triton→CUDA switch, with 52/54 formal workloads passing at the end of the published trace but no complete formal mean reported; a sourced cryopreservation literature report), Creation (MiniOS built in ~20 minutes with persistence/autorun verified across two QEMU sessions; CAD assemblies for an engine, robot, joint module, rocket), Delivery (professional reports on clean-energy siting, healthcare investment, semiconductor capital allocation), and Cybersecurity (an authorized, isolated-environment vulnerability diagnosis-repair-reverification case) — explicitly presented as illustrative rather than average-case success-rate evidence.

**Human–AI collaboration study (Sec. 4).** 769 task-review records from 56 participants plus agent logs; 739 had a clear AI-use response, of which 713 (96.5%) involved AI. A 22-participant cohort's daily median ratio of logged agent actions to human prompts (each ratio = 7-day trailing total) rose from 11.0 to 28.5 over Aug 7–Sep 4, 2026, with widening interquartile range. Task-feasibility-without-AI was asked for 455 completed AI-assisted tasks with usable responses (151, 33.2%, from 27 of 56 participants, rated infeasible without AI under matched scope/quality/resource constraints). Decision attribution (proposer vs. selector) was recorded for goals/scope, methods/parameters, and acceptance criteria among participants in execution roles; difficulty-resolution mode was recorded for 588 tasks with a usable response; output disposition and revision-actor were recorded for 627 and 354 tasks respectively.

**Results.** AI proposed methods/parameters in 55.4% of decisions but only 16.9% of goals/scope decisions — a >3× swing — while AI's share of *final* decisions stayed within 6.1%–9.2% across all three decision types (humans: 85.5% method/parameter, 93.4% goals/scope, 81.9% acceptance-criteria final say); within the infeasible-without-AI subset, humans made the final goal choice in 95.4% of cases (144/151), a higher rate than in general project decisions. Of 588 difficulty-resolution cases, 76.0% advanced via human intervention (informational context/clarification 35.2% + diagnosis/method-change 34.7%, versus direct partial-edit 3.2% + takeover 0.7%), agents self-recovered in 23.0%, and 1.0% went unresolved — human help was "about eighteen times more likely to change what the agent knew than to change who did the work." Of 627 outputs with known disposition, 354 (56.5%) received substantive revision; of those, AI revised after human feedback in 75.4% of cases versus direct human editing in 19.2%.

# Q9 Contributions

1. Atria Dawn Preview, a 744B-parameter MoE agentic language model trained via a Verifiable Experience Pipeline that grounds every training task in a real, tool-mediated execution environment with an externally checked outcome, achieving the top reported score on 5 of 16 evaluated agentic benchmarks and competitive-to-leading results on the rest.
2. A trajectory-curation and failure-driven task-construction methodology that filters invalid rollouts while preserving task→trajectory→artifact→verification links, and explicitly reuses independently-verified failed runs as diagnostic training signal.
3. A quantitative, decision-attributed empirical account of one large real AI-development project (769 task records, 56 participants), separating who proposes an option from who selects it and who diagnoses a difficulty from who executes its fix — showing AI's share of proposals is highly domain-dependent (16.9%–55.4%) while its share of final decisions stays narrowly bounded (6.1%–9.2%) regardless of domain.
4. Evidence that a rising agent-actions-per-human-prompt ratio (11.0→28.5 over 4 weeks) reflects growing execution delegation rather than growing decision-making autonomy, since human final-decision share and human-intervention rates on difficulties remained high and largely stable across the same period.
5. A 4-stage taxonomy of human–AI R&D roles (research object → task runner → project-level coworker → speculative RSI) and four named open challenges for reaching genuine RSI — diverse-direction generation with pre-result value assessment, converting accumulated experience into intrinsic (not merely retrieved) research capability, maintaining meaningful human oversight as agent-action volume scales, and establishing authority-allocation/alignment protocols — framed from the paper's own development experience rather than derived from a formal cross-domain benchmark analysis.

# Q10 Next Steps

The paper's own stated limitations/open challenges (Sec. 5): it explicitly does not resolve how to keep AI research directions diverse and pre-assess their value before results are available (noting agent proposals in its own project concentrated in variations within researcher-fixed directions); how to make agents retain and generalize lessons from failures across sessions rather than re-diagnosing the same issues repeatedly (the paper's own data shows humans, not agents, currently carry this cross-session memory); how to preserve meaningful human oversight as the volume of agent-generated work outpaces what one person can inspect closely; and what authority-allocation and alignment protocols should govern increasingly autonomous AI R&D, explicitly rejecting the idea that AI should be granted safety-relevant permissions "in the same manner as other permissions."

Beyond the paper's own framing, three points are worth flagging for a reader assessing this note's evidence. First, because the collaboration study is entirely internal to one project and one (evidently well-resourced, multi-institution) research organization, it is not established whether the observed proposal/selection split (AI proposes more on narrow method/parameter decisions, humans retain goal-setting) reflects something structural about current-generation agentic capability or something specific to this team's task design, tooling choices, and review culture — a smaller or differently-organized team might show a different split. Second, the paper's four open challenges are stated as the authors' own assessment rather than derived from a systematic comparison against, e.g., the five-level autonomy taxonomy and Headroom-Closed Index in [The Last AI Built by Humans](2609.11873.md) — mapping Atria Dawn's own reported data (85.5% human final-decision share on methods, 93.4% on goals) onto that taxonomy's levels would be a natural follow-up this note's evidence does not itself attempt. Third, the released benchmark comparison table's "highest reported score on five" framing is sensitive to which six comparison models were included and when their own scores were last updated (the source website, not the arXiv paper, is the primary artifact and was not independently checked here) — a reader relying on this note for a ranking claim should treat the specific numeric margins as provisional pending independent verification.
