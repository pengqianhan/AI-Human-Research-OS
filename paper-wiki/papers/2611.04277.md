# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a policy model (Qwen3.5-4B or 9B), a base harness H0 (DeepSeek Harness, DSH), training and validation tasks with binary verifiers, and an LLM harness editor.
* **Output (Y):** one updated policy that does better *under the original H0*, so the extra execution support used in training is not needed at deployment. A by-product is a reusable pool of sibling harnesses with a router that can also pick one per task at inference.
* **Constraints:** sibling harnesses must keep H0's execution foundation, environment interfaces, and verifier; the pool is frozen during policy training; only policy tokens enter the loss.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. Automated harness optimization (Meta-Harness, ADAS, GEPA, Darwin Gödel Machine) and joint harness-policy methods (WHALE, HarnessForge, Co-Harness, HarnessX, HarnessBandit) already exist. The paper's new angle is that the policy has to work without the harness that helped it. It motivates this with three properties it measures on AppWorld with Qwen3.5-4B: *locality* (an edit repairs some failures and breaks some solved tasks), *complementarity* (siblings recover one another's errors, and their union covers more tasks than any one sibling or H0 resampling at the same rollout count), and *nonstationarity* (a harness's advantage over H0 can turn into a disadvantage as the policy trains, and rankings can reverse). From these it argues that merging repairs into one best harness, or re-evolving harnesses alongside the policy, is the wrong target.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If siblings that repair different failures are used only on tasks where every H0 rollout fails, then the same policy's successful rollouts under them restore the reward contrast GRPO needs. The behaviors those rollouts reinforce should then remain when the policy runs under H0 again. Under that hypothesis, the H0-evaluated policy should beat resampling under H0 at similar sampling cost, and the router should prefer H0 more often after training.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Harness evolution.** ReAct and SWE-agent show that the execution layer matters; prompt and program optimizers include OPRO, TextGrad, EvoPrompt, Promptbreeder, DSPy, and [GEPA](../concepts/gepa.md); system-level search includes ADAS, AFlow, the [Darwin Gödel Machine](../concepts/darwin-godel-machine.md), [Meta-Harness](../concepts/meta-harness.md), and Life-Harness; [RRSI](2609.24972.md) regularizes harness edits toward transferable mechanisms.
* **Harness-policy optimization.** WHALE (alternating harness search with rejection-sampling fine-tuning), HarnessForge (branch-specific harness-policy pairs), Co-Harness, HarnessX (cross-harness GRPO over a shared replay buffer), and HarnessBandit (one harness per optimizer step chosen by learnability and transferability signals).
* **Agent RL and sparse rewards.** [GRPO](../concepts/grpo.md), DAPO's dynamic sampling of mixed groups, RAGEN, LOOP on AppWorld, AgentGym-RL, [Agent Lightning](2508.03680.md), and classical tools for rare success (Hindsight Experience Replay, RUDDER, Go-Explore).
* **Knowledge transfer.** Policy distillation, kickstarting, expert iteration, STaR, DAgger, GKD, and learning with privileged information (Learning by Cheating), which is the closest analogy: the helper harness is training-time support the deployed policy will not have.
* **Key people.** Kangning Zhang (first author) with Weiwen Liu and Weinan Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University) and Wenxiang Jiao and Yuan Lu (Xiaohongshu) as corresponding authors; co-authors at the Zhongguancun Institute of Artificial Intelligence, Southeast University, and Carnegie Mellon. Likely critics: the WHALE and HarnessForge authors, whose methods appear here as reimplementations, and Meta-Harness authors, who would ask how a co-evolved harness compares when it is evaluated in its own harness rather than under H0.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

**Directional harness evolution.** Starting from H0, each round runs the parents on training tasks, groups failures into repair directions (two or three related root causes, each seen in at least two failed tasks), and has an editor (GLM-5.3) write one child per direction. Edits may touch prompts, skills, context, memory, hooks, or the tool view. The next parents (at most four) are chosen to maximize the *union* of validation tasks solved, so a parent that covers others' failures counts more than one with a high individual score. Every executable, protocol-compliant child is kept. After three rounds the pool holds H0 plus 19 siblings and is frozen. Appendix D shows typical edits: a hook that warns to keep paginating until a page is short or empty, a rule to intersect conditions instead of widening to the containing collection, and a low-budget reminder to reserve a step for `complete_task`.

**Complementary harness rescue training.** For each task the policy draws K = 8 rollouts under H0. If at least one succeeds, the group trains as usual. If all fail, the router picks eight siblings, the same policy makes one fresh attempt under each from the task's initial state, and that group replaces the failed one. GRPO then uses group-relative advantages with asymmetric clipping (0.20 and 0.28), no KL term, a truncated correction for rollout and training backends that assign different probabilities to the same token, and a loss averaged over policy tokens per task. Harness-injected text and tool observations are masked out of the loss.

**Adaptive harness router.** For each sibling, an LLM compares paired validation trajectories (sibling against H0) and writes a *behavior profile* of prefer and avoid entries, each a When condition and a Behavior. Several LLMs then rank four candidates per task from the profiles, validation success rates, and outcomes on similar tasks, and the votes are summed with weights 1, 3/4, 1/2, and 1/4. *Policy-aware re-anchoring* rebuilds the profiles with the frozen current policy every ten updates, because a sibling's usefulness changes as the policy learns. At inference the same router can pick one harness per task from all 20, H0 included ("Rank-1"), before any attempt is made.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Benchmarks.** [AppWorld](../concepts/appworld.md) (320 train, 150 validation, 262 test), DBBench (1,600, 200, 300), and [SWE-Gym](../concepts/swe-gym.md) (240, 100, 160). Training tasks drive harness editing and policy updates; validation tasks drive parent selection and profiles; test tasks are held out.
* **Metrics.** Pass@3 (solved at least once in three rollouts; see [Pass@K](../concepts/pass-at-k.md)) and Avg@3, reported as means over three randomized runs, with standard deviations for Harness-S only.
* **Comparisons, all from the same Qwen3.5 starting policy and base harness.** Plain GRPO; GRPO with H0 rescue (an all-failure group is resampled under H0 with the same per-task allocation); two fixed mixtures (four DSH plus four Claude Code rollouts, or DSH plus four siblings, on every task); Meta-Harness with a frozen policy; Harness-S with a frozen policy (harness evolution only); and reimplemented WHALE and HarnessForge. Meta-Harness, WHALE, and HarnessForge are evaluated with their selected best harness, Harness-S under H0 and under Rank-1 routing. GPT-6-Sol, DeepSeek-v4-Flash, and GLM-5.3-Flash under H0 are reference points.
* **Mechanism and ablations (mostly AppWorld, 4B).** Effective-group rate and token share across training (Figure 3); rescue allocation variants at a fixed budget of eight rescue rollouts (random, rule router without profiles, four harnesses × two rollouts, fixed-profile Top-8, adaptive Top-8; Table 2); LLM-labelled behavior frequencies under H0 on AppWorld and SWE-Gym (Table 3); two traced transfer cases (Table 4); router rank before and after training (Table 5); transfer to Claude Code and OpenClaw harnesses (Figure 4); and cost (Tables 9 and 10).

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * *Better under H0, beyond extra sampling.* Under H0, Harness-S beats both plain GRPO and GRPO with H0 rescue in all 12 benchmark, size, and metric cells (reader count). At 4B on AppWorld it reaches 67.56 Pass@3 and 56.87 Avg@3, against 60.31 and 48.98 for GRPO and 61.83 and 51.91 for H0 rescue. The rescue control uses more rollouts than Harness-S (3,616 against 3,032) and more GPU time (97.77 against 67.19 GPU-hours) and still scores lower. This cost comparison is for AppWorld 4B only.
  * *Ahead of the co-evolution baselines without their help at test time.* At 9B, Harness-S under H0 (72.52, 40.00, and 76.33 Pass@3 on AppWorld, SWE-Gym, and DBBench) beats WHALE under its own best harness (68.70, 33.75, and 74.00). With Rank-1 routing it reaches 78.00 Pass@3 on DBBench, above the three frontier references there (at most 76.67), though not on AppWorld or SWE-Gym.
  * *More usable groups.* The share of groups with both successes and failures is 79.38% over the run, against 70.94% for H0 rescue and 64.69% for GRPO, and the matching token share is 80.88% against 72.68%. Validation success under H0 ends at 63.33% against 54.00% and 48.00%. Both fixed mixtures finish below plain GRPO, so harness diversity helps when aimed at failed tasks, not when spread over all of them.
  * *Re-anchoring matters.* At the same eight rescue rollouts, adaptive profiles beat fixed profiles in all four training stages and add 4.71 points of final Avg@3.
  * *Less reliance on helpers after training.* The router puts H0 first on 22.52% of tasks after training, against 1.53% before, and Rank-1's margin over H0 shrinks from 13.49 to 4.58 Avg@3 points.
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * *The reported means look like single-run counts.* With 262, 160, and 300 test tasks, one run's Pass@3 moves in steps of 1/N and its Avg@3 in steps of 1/(3N); a mean of three runs would land on that grid only about a third of the time. 149 of the 150 cells in Table 1 sit exactly on it, which fits single runs or a median better than the stated mean of three (reader check). The one exception, the base 4B model's 42.62 AppWorld Pass@3, also disagrees with the 14.21-point Rank-1 margin quoted in Section 4.6 (56.87 minus 42.62 is 14.25). The ± values belong to Harness-S only; baselines have no spread.
  * *The SWE-Gym gain is mostly breadth.* At 4B, Pass@3 rises 14.37 points over GRPO but Avg@3 only 6.25, and Harness-S has a 10-point gap between the two metrics where GRPO has under 2 (reader calculation). The policy gets better at finding a fix in one of three attempts than at fixing the task every time.
  * *Per-stage rescue rates rest on few tasks.* Table 9's rollout counts imply eight tasks per update (2,560 rollouts over 40 updates in groups of eight), and Harness-S's 472 extra rollouts imply about 59 rescued tasks in the whole run, roughly 15 per ten-update stage (reader arithmetic). Table 2's stage-by-stage differences therefore carry wide uncertainty.
  * *Validation data does several jobs.* The same validation tasks choose parents, build profiles, and give the Figure 3c learning curves; the second transfer case is a validation instance. Only Table 1 and the behavior frequencies use held-out test tasks.
  * *Transfer evidence is behavioral and anecdotal.* Behavior frequencies come from an unnamed LLM labelling trajectories, and the transfer cases are two hand-picked AppWorld examples. They are consistent with internalization but do not show that rescued trajectories cause the H0 behavior, which would need an ablation that drops rescued groups.
  * *Mostly one setting.* Mechanism, ablation, router, and cost analyses are AppWorld with the 4B model. Cross-harness transfer is shown only in a figure, and the trained policy scores lower under Claude Code and OpenClaw than under DSH.
  * *Baselines are adaptations.* WHALE and HarnessForge are reimplemented inside the common framework, and the frozen-policy comparison uses an adapted Meta-Harness, so differences from the original systems are possible.
  * *Rank-1 is not free.* "Same executor rollout count" leaves out the router's multi-LLM voting calls per task at inference, which the API-token totals in Table 10 count only for the full pipeline (reader observation).

# Q9 Contributions

* A measured case that harness variants from one base are local, complementary, and nonstationary, with the design consequence that a pool beats a single merged harness.
* A training recipe that uses the pool only to rescue all-failure groups and evaluates the result without the pool, separating retained policy gains from inference-time support.
* A routing method that describes each sibling by policy-relative behavior profiles and refreshes them as the policy changes, reusable at inference.
* Costed comparisons against resampling, fixed mixtures, frozen-policy harness search, and reimplemented co-evolution baselines.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Pool reuse and scaling (from the paper).** Reusing a pool across policies, exchanging task state between independently built harnesses, and measuring how added repair diversity changes gains and calibration cost.
* **A causal transfer test (reader suggestion).** Train with rescued groups dropped, or with rescue groups drawn under H0 at matched counts and success rates, and compare H0 behavior frequencies, to separate learning from sibling behavior from simply having more mixed groups.
* **Report per-run results (reader suggestion).** Publishing the three runs per cell, with baselines' spreads, would settle the granularity question and the significance of gaps of a few points.
* **Cheaper routing (reader suggestion).** A small classifier distilled from the LLM votes would show how much of Rank-1's remaining Avg@3 margin survives without multi-LLM calls per task.
* **Harness-specific behaviors (reader suggestion).** The behaviors that transfer here (schema lookup, pagination, read-back, assertion checks) look like general procedures. Testing whether siblings that add new *tools*, rather than reminders and rules, also transfer would show the method's limit.
