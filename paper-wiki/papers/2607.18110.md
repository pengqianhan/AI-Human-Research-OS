# Q1 Problem Definition

The paper addresses post-training a language model policy on **non-verifiable open-ended tasks** — explanation, recommendation, creative writing, general instruction following — where no ground-truth answer exists and quality is multi-dimensional (factuality, relevance, completeness, organization, style).

* Input `X`: a prompt `x` plus a set of prompt-specific rubrics `R_x` that decompose quality into independently checkable criteria; a policy `π_θ` samples a response `y`.
* Output `Y`: updated policy parameters `θ`.
* Constraint: the only quality signal comes from another language model `M` evaluating `(x, y, R_x)`. Neither `M` nor any extra context may be required at inference time.

The specific defect targeted is an **information bottleneck**. Standard RL keeps only the scalar score from `M` and discards the textual analysis, so responses assigned the same score become indistinguishable to the optimizer. The authors argue this bites hardest near the top of the scale, where many satisfactory responses share the maximum score despite differing in ways the evaluator can articulate.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The task setting (rubric-based post-training on open-ended prompts) is not new — the paper builds directly on Rubrics-as-Rewards and the LLM-as-a-Judge line. What is framed as new is the diagnosis: treating the *width of the feedback channel*, rather than the *quality of the evaluator*, as the limiting factor. The paper is explicit that these are separable concerns and that improving judge calibration remains a complementary direction it does not address.

The framing matters because rubric-based RL is becoming the default recipe for the large fraction of post-training data that has no verifier, so a structural limitation there propagates widely.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If the feedback model's rubric-based assessment is distilled into transferable natural-language *experiential knowledge* and used to condition a teacher whose token-level distribution the policy is trained to match — instead of being compressed into a scalar reward — then the policy will (a) reach higher scores on held-out open-ended evaluation, (b) generalize better to unseen benchmarks, and (c) be less susceptible to training-set reward overoptimization.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

Authors are from Microsoft Research (Tianzhu Ye, Li Dong, Furu Wei and colleagues); the work sits directly on top of the same group's *On-Policy Context Distillation for Language Models* (arXiv 2602.12275) and *Online Experiential Learning for Language Models* (arXiv 2603.16856).

The related-work taxonomy in the paper:

* **Learning from experience** — Silver and Sutton's "era of experience"; Reflexion; ExpEL; TextGrad; GEPA; Feedback Descent; Critique-GRPO. The paper's stated objection is that these either keep experience in external memory / prompts, or fold critiques back into an objective still bottlenecked by a scalar reward.
* **Context distillation and self-distillation** — Askell et al.; Snell et al.; MiniLLM; on-policy distillation (Thinking Machines); on-policy context distillation. A large cluster of 2026 self-distillation work is cited, including analyses of when self-distillation *degrades* reasoning.
* **RL and judging** — Sutton and Barto; GRPO / DeepSeekMath; MT-Bench LLM-as-a-Judge; G-Eval; Rubrics-as-Rewards; reward gaming (Skalse et al.).

Likely sympathetic readers: the on-policy distillation community. Likely critics: RLHF researchers who would question whether the comparison against a GRPO rubric-reward baseline is tuned strongly enough, and whether "bandwidth" is doing real explanatory work.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

Repurpose the *same* feedback model `M` under the *same* rubrics from a **judge** into a **coach**:

1. Sample on-policy responses `y ~ π_θ(·|x)`.
2. The coach produces `e = Extract_Knowledge(M(x, y, R_x))` — general, transferable guidance for handling similar tasks, extracted from `<experience>` tags in its output, rather than instance-specific corrections.
3. `e` is prepended as context to a teacher `π_teacher`, inducing a shifted distribution.
4. Minimize token-level **reverse KL** between `π_θ(·|x, y_<t)` and `π_teacher(·|e, x, y_<t)` along the policy's own samples.

The teacher is either the initial frozen policy checkpoint (default) or the previous epoch's checkpoint. Because the knowledge is consolidated into weights, neither coach nor context is needed at inference.

The differentiator versus critique-conditioned RL is the *optimization target*, which the paper's discussion makes explicit: RL is **reward-seeking** (mass concentrates on the highest-reward responses, so any feature correlated with reward is exploitable); EL is **distribution-matching** (it stays close to the teacher's distributional shape).

Supporting the design is a **feedback-bandwidth intuition**: a 1–10 discrete score carries at most log₂10 ≈ 3.3 bits, a bf16 reward head at most 16 bits, while a 1024-token context over a 150k vocabulary has a theoretical ceiling of ≈17,600 bits — >1,000× a bf16 scalar and >5,000× a 1–10 score. The paper repeatedly flags this as a channel-capacity upper bound, **not** a measure of usable supervision, since natural-language redundancy and imperfect extraction shrink the effective signal.

# Q6 Experiment Design

Training prompts: WildChat-IF (7,500 curated real user queries), with per-prompt rubrics pre-generated by GPT-4o. Policies: Qwen3-8B (non-thinking) and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct. Feedback model: either the initial frozen policy checkpoint (self-feedback) or GPT-4o.

Baseline: Rubric-as-Reward implemented with GRPO using the 1–10 judge rating as scalar reward. Both methods share batch size 256 prompts × 8 responses, lr 1e-6, prompt/response caps 8192/4096, temperature 1, 3 epochs, checkpoints every 10 steps. Reverse KL is computed over the top-256 student-ranked vocabulary tokens without renormalization.

Evaluation: 250 held-out WildChat-IF prompts (4 samples each, GPT-4o rubric-conditioned judge) plus four unseen benchmarks — AlpacaEval v2.0 and ArenaHard v2.0 (win rate vs GPT-4-Turbo), WildBench (preference score in [-100, 100]), CreativeWritingV3 (direct rubric score in [0, 100]). IFEval serves as the out-of-distribution capability check. Reported numbers average the top-3 checkpoints.

The design covers the three hypothesis claims reasonably well: two policy families × two feedback sources for (a), a train-subset-versus-test comparison for (c), and unseen benchmarks for (b). Two ablations isolate the mechanism — teacher update strategy, and teacher context format — plus a synthetic distribution-matching study.

Design weaknesses: a single evaluator family (GPT-4o) generates rubrics, judges RL rewards, *and* scores evaluation, so evaluator idiosyncrasies are shared across the pipeline; the authors report a cross-check with a stronger proprietary evaluator showing consistent relative trends but do not publish those numbers. No human evaluation. No variance or seed information is reported for the main table.

# Q7 Datasets and Code

* **Training**: WildChat-IF (7,500 prompts) with GPT-4o-generated rubrics; Tulu3 prompts mixed at 1:0.25 in the iterative-teacher setting, filtered to exclude WildChat-overlapping SFT sources.
* **Evaluation**: WildChat-IF held-out 250; AlpacaEval v2.0; WildBench; ArenaHard v2.0; CreativeWritingV3 (EQ-Bench); IFEval for OOD.
* **Models**: Qwen3-8B, OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-1.7B (policy) / Qwen3-4B (coach) for the teacher-context ablation; GPT-4o via API.
* **Code**: released at [aka.ms/el-code](https://aka.ms/el-code) (link stated in the paper; not verified here).

Full reproduction requires paid GPT-4o access for both the rubrics and every evaluation, which is the main practical barrier.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

The main result (Table 2) is consistent in direction but modest in magnitude. Qwen3-8B with self-feedback: WildChat 78.1 base → 79.2 RL → 80.0 EL; AlpacaEval 34.5 → 37.3 → 40.0; WildBench 17.6 → 18.4 → 21.8; ArenaHard 29.1 → 30.5 → 31.0; CW-v3 73.8 → 74.3 → 76.0. OLMo-3-7B shows the largest transfer gains (AlpacaEval 45.9 → 50.8, WildBench 42.1 → 47.5 with self-feedback). The one place EL loses is Qwen3-8B + GPT-4o on AlpacaEval (37.4 vs RL's 38.2) — the paper's "outperforms on most benchmarks" phrasing is accurate rather than overstated.

The **generalization claim is the strongest evidence**: EL gains *less* than RL on a 1,000-example training subset yet more on the WildChat test set, AlpacaEval, and WildBench (Figure 3). Training-set gain without transfer is a recognizable reward-overoptimization signature, and this pattern is hard to explain by EL simply being a better optimizer. The reward-hacking claim is therefore supported as a behavioral observation, though the paper offers no direct diagnosis of *what* the RL policy learned to exploit.

The **teacher-context ablation is the most mechanistically informative** result (Qwen3-1.7B, Table 4): base 64.8 / IFEval 68.0; RL 67.7 / 67.6; Full Critique 67.9 / 64.6; Rubrics Only 68.1 / 65.6; Multiple-Choice 66.3 / 68.5; default EL 68.6 / 68.8. Two mechanisms separate cleanly here. Evaluation-flavored context (full critique, bare rubrics) biases the teacher toward a *critiquing* distribution that the policy imitates, damaging OOD instruction following. Multiple-Choice — deliberately constructed at ≈3.3 bits, matching the scalar reward — preserves OOD accuracy but yields the smallest gain, which is the closest thing in the paper to a controlled test of the bandwidth argument. Extraction of transferable knowledge, not merely "more text," is what wins.

The iterative-teacher ablation shows a real cost: WildChat 80.0 → 80.7 but IFEval collapses 83.1 → 74.9, recovered to 79.9 by mixing general Tulu3 prompts with a frozen-checkpoint teacher. That is honest reporting of a forgetting failure mode rather than a clean win.

**Where the evidence is thin.** The bandwidth argument is the paper's headline intuition and it is *not* measured — the 17,600-bit figure is a channel-capacity ceiling, and the paper says so in a footnote and again in the main text. The distribution-matching study (Section 3.5) is a 200-token categorical toy with a hand-constructed quantized reward, and the authors state it is neither a simulation of the pipelines nor a proof of a limitation of all scalar-reward methods; it shows that *this particular* five-level quantization produces a staircase where distributional matching recovers a smooth bimodal target. Treated as illustration it is fine; it should not be cited as evidence about RL in general. Model scale tops out at 8B, and one configuration (including the response alongside the full scoring output in teacher context) diverged within a few steps — noted but not analyzed.

Net: the hypothesis is supported for the tested setting — 7–8B policies, rubric-scored open-ended instruction following, GPT-4o-family evaluation — with the causal story about *why* resting on a plausible but unmeasured bandwidth intuition plus one suggestive controlled ablation.

# Q9 Contributions

1. A named reframing — judge versus coach — that separates the *bandwidth* of the feedback channel from the *quality* of the feedback model, and a clean statement of why reward-seeking and distribution-matching objectives behave differently under a fallible evaluator.
2. A concrete method (EL) combining coach-extracted transferable experiential knowledge with on-policy context distillation, which leaves nothing to carry at inference time.
3. Evidence that textual experiential supervision transfers better than scalar rubric rewards across two policy families and two feedback sources, with the training-versus-transfer gap as the load-bearing observation.
4. An ablation separating three confounds that a coarser experiment would conflate: knowledge *extraction* versus raw critique, context *format* versus context *volume*, and bandwidth versus content.
5. Documentation of two failure modes — evaluation-flavored teacher context inducing critique imitation, and iterative teachers causing OOD forgetting — with a working stabilizer (general-prompt OPD from the frozen checkpoint).

# Q10 Next Steps

Limitations the paper acknowledges: bandwidth is an unmeasured upper bound; the toy analysis is illustrative only; EL addresses channel width, not evaluator bias, so a miscalibrated coach still misdirects training; iterative teachers forget without a general-prompt stabilizer.

Limitations it does not foreground: the ≤8B scale; the single evaluator family spanning rubric generation, RL reward, and final scoring; no human evaluation; no reported variance across seeds; and no cost accounting for coach inference (the paper argues method-specific update cost is small relative to shared rollout and feedback generation, but coach calls themselves are part of that shared cost, so EL is not cheaper than RL — only comparably expensive).

Directions worth pursuing:

* **Measure the channel instead of bounding it.** The Multiple-Choice ablation is a bandwidth *dial*; sweeping it (4, 16, 64, 256 directives; truncated experience contexts) would turn the paper's central intuition into a curve.
* **Decouple evaluator from judge.** Rubrics from one model family, coaching from a second, evaluation from a third would test whether gains survive when the pipeline stops sharing latent preferences — the same measurement-validity concern raised by [AI Agents Do Not Fail Alone](2607.14275.md).
* **Coach calibration.** Since EL is explicitly orthogonal to feedback quality, pairing it with a better-calibrated coach should compose; whether the gains add or interfere is untested.
* **Transfer to agentic settings.** Experiential knowledge distilled from rubric assessments is close in spirit to the retrieved experience banks in [MemoHarness](2607.14159.md) and the skill libraries in [OpenSkill](2606.06741.md) — the open question is whether experience is better *retrieved at inference* or *consolidated into weights*, and this paper argues for consolidation only in the single-turn open-ended case.

For this repository, the relevant reading is the judge-versus-coach distinction rather than the training recipe: any loop that compresses a rich model critique into a scalar before acting on it is discarding most of what the critic knew.
