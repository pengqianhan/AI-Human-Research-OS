# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a discovery domain with a fixed coding/discovery agent and a fixed task-scoring evaluator; the exploration policy's per-round observation is a discovery tree 𝒯 rooted at the initial workspace state, where each node records a generation-evaluation attempt's resulting filesystem snapshot, generated artifact, evaluation diagnostics, and score.
* **Output (Y):** an executable exploration-policy program π that, at each decision round, selects a batch of eligible tree nodes (the root, or current leaves) from which to launch up to W parallel new attempts — i.e., policy code controlling branching, parallelism, and stopping, not the discovered artifacts themselves; downstream, the discovered programs/kernels/mathematical constructions the policy's online rollouts ultimately produce.
* **Core problem:** long-horizon agent-driven discovery (thousands of proposal-evaluation cycles) makes exploration-strategy quality the binding constraint on efficiency, but improving the exploration policy itself is a meta-level optimization problem with two structural bottlenecks — feedback is delayed and expensive (assessing a policy requires observing how it shapes an entire subsequent discovery rollout, not scoring one candidate) and the space of possible policies is vast (many proposed revisions may perform poorly before a good one is found) — so meta-policy improvement has historically required many costly online rollouts to close the loop.
* **Scope:** three domains (algorithm engineering: Lasso regularization-path solvers; mathematical optimization: Sum-Difference, Circle Packing, Autocorrelation Inequalities; GPU kernel engineering: KernelBench VGG16/LayerNorm/ConvDiv/ConvMax), using Gemini-3.1 Pro and Gemini-3.7-Flash via the Gemini CLI as the underlying fixed coding/discovery agent; the paper does not evaluate domains outside these three, nor does it modify the discovery agent, evaluator, or execution interfaces — only the exploration-policy code changes.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The paper does not claim to originate LLM-driven evolutionary discovery (AlphaEvolve, OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, SimpleTES and related systems already iterate propose-evaluate-refine loops) or the general idea that exploration itself can be optimized rather than held fixed (EvoX explicitly optimizes search strategies; SkyDiscover and SwarmResearch provide adaptive/dynamic exploration infrastructure). Its stated novelty is a specific mechanism for making meta-level policy improvement cheap: rather than treating a completed discovery history as static textual context (as in prior memory/context work) or as gradient-based fine-tuning data, the paper reconceptualizes a finished discovery tree as a *replay simulator* — because every node's execution outcome is already recorded, a new policy can be evaluated by replaying it against the tree (revealing only nodes the policy's choices would visit, in recorded parent-child order) at zero additional execution cost, drawing an explicit analogy to model-based reinforcement learning and the Dreamer family of World Models (Ha & Schmidhuber; Hafner et al.), applied here to *meta*-level exploration-policy improvement rather than object-level task policy improvement.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If a completed online discovery rollout's tree of recorded (node, action, outcome) triples is treated as an empirical, replayable model of the portion of the discovery space already observed, then a policy-development agent can "dream" — construct and evaluate many candidate exploration-policy revisions entirely offline by replaying each against the accumulated history, using a replay objective that rewards discovery quality while penalizing execution cost and rewarding useful parallelism — and the policy selected by this offline process (guaranteed no worse than the current policy on average replay score, since the current policy is always included as a candidate) will, when redeployed online, produce a discovery trajectory that dominates a fixed-policy baseline on the quality-versus-compute frontier, because the meta-level feedback loop has been converted from expensive multi-rollout online trial-and-error into fast, zero-execution-cost offline simulation.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **AI-driven scientific and algorithmic discovery.** AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025), OpenEvolve (Sharma, 2025), CodeEvolve (Assumpção et al., 2025), ShinkaEvolve (Lange et al., 2026), PACEvolve, DeltaEvolve, MLEvolve are cited as the base iterative generate-evaluate-refine paradigm; SkyDiscover (adaptive discovery infrastructure) and SwarmResearch (dynamic multi-branch orchestration) are cited as recognizing exploration itself needs infrastructure; EvoX is cited as the closest prior work to explicitly optimize search strategies rather than only candidate solutions — the paper's stated gap relative to all of these is that useful supervision for exploration-policy quality is itself expensive and delayed, which none directly addresses via offline replay.
* **Self-evolving agents.** Distinguishes object-level self-evolution (evolving model weights, agent harnesses, contexts, skills, rubrics, or environments — citing G-Zero, EnvHarness (this wiki's [EnvHarness](2608.19880.md)), agentic context engineering, and others) from meta-level self-evolution (optimizing search strategies or self-improvement procedures themselves — citing EvoX, HGM/Huxley-Gödel-Machine-style work, Hyperagents). The paper positions Dream-RSI specifically in this second, less-populated category, arguing meta-level strategies are "difficult to improve because their quality is often revealed only after costly long-horizon rollouts" — the exact bottleneck its replay-simulator mechanism targets.
* **Memory, history, and experience reuse.** Contrasts with DeltaEvolve (structures evolutionary history via semantic deltas), SwarmResearch/MLEvolve (cross-branch or retrospective guidance for subsequent search), and context/harness/skill-memory work (agentic context engineering, this wiki's [Prime Agent](2608.23552.md) via Karten et al., 2026, cited for evolving agent harnesses) — Dream-RSI's differentiator is using history as an *interactive replay simulator* many alternative policies can be tested against, not as passive context or memory a single ongoing policy consults.
* **World models / model-based RL.** Explicitly draws the paper's central analogy from Ha & Schmidhuber's "World Models" and the Dreamer family (Hafner et al., 2019/2020/2023/2025) — learning a compact dynamics model from experience and improving a policy by imagining trajectories within it — applied here at the meta-exploration-policy level using an *exact* replay of recorded history rather than a learned approximate dynamics model.
* **Key people.** Tong Zheng (University of Maryland, College Park) is first author; co-authors span University of Maryland, Google DeepMind (Zhankui He, Benjamin Coleman, Di Bai, Wang-Cheng Kang among others), and University of Virginia (Haolin Liu). # Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Discovery trees as the shared decision interface.** A tree 𝒯 rooted at the initial workspace state r; each non-root node v has one primary parent (root or an earlier node), from which the discovery agent resumes to produce a new generation-evaluation attempt, recording the resulting artifact, diagnostics, and score s_v. Both online execution and offline replay share the same interface: the policy observes the current tree, the set of eligible nodes A(𝒯) = {root} ∪ {current leaves}, and selects a batch C of up to W nodes to continue from.
2. **Online rollout.** At outer iteration t, the current policy π_t guides a fresh online rollout (with access to prior completed history for context but a separate new tree being built): at each round, the policy's chosen batch is executed in parallel by the fixed discovery agent and scored by the fixed evaluator, producing new children; the rollout ends on an empty batch or a round cap K₁, and the resulting tree 𝒯_t is appended to the accumulated history.
3. **Offline replay ("dreaming").** With history fixed, M candidate policy revisions π_t^0,...,π_t^{M-1} (starting from the current policy) are each evaluated by replaying against every historical tree: replay deterministically reveals a selected node's *already-recorded* child rather than generating a new one, so evaluating a policy version requires no new discovery-agent or evaluator calls — only reading stored records, up to a round cap K₂ or until the entire recorded tree is revealed.
4. **Replay objective balancing quality, cost, and parallelism.** For each policy-tree pair, the replay score V = (best score seen along the revealed subtree) − β₁·(number of revealed nodes) + β₂·(revealed nodes ÷ number of decision rounds) — rewarding discovery quality, penalizing total attempted generations, and rewarding batching useful continuations in parallel rather than issuing them sequentially.
5. **Policy-development agent and monotonic-non-regression selection.** An LLM-based policy-development agent examines each version's replay trajectories/scores plus feedback from earlier revisions to identify successful decisions and recurring failures, then revises the executable policy code; because the candidate set always includes the unmodified current policy (m=0), the version selected for the next online rollout is guaranteed no worse than the current policy in average replay score on the fixed history — only the exploration-policy code changes across iterations; the discovery agent, evaluator, and execution interfaces stay fixed throughout.

# Q6 Experiment Design

**Controlled baseline.** "Recursive Fixed Exploration" — identical discovery agent, evaluator, initialization, per-round resource budget, and starting policy (a manually-designed parallel-refining strategy: multiple independent parallel workspaces, each repeatedly refining its own local trajectory) as Dream-RSI, but the exploration policy stays static across all recursive rounds rather than being dreamed-over and redeployed; both methods therefore behave identically in round 1 and diverge only from round 2 onward. Discovery cost is measured as cumulative discovery-agent calls.

**Algorithm engineering — Lasso regularization path (Sec. 4.1).** Following the SimpleTES benchmark setting (17 synthetic training instances varying dimensionality/sparsity/correlation/active-set structure; six held-out downstream datasets — Gisette, RCV1, DNA, Leukemia, Colon, Duke Breast — spanning non-biological and biological domains), 5 rounds each with Gemini-3.1 Pro (10 parallel workspaces × 11 refinement steps = 110 calls/round under Fixed Exploration) and Gemini-3.7-Flash (32 × 20 = 640 calls/round). Result: with Gemini-3.1 Pro, Dream-RSI cuts mean held-out runtime 3587.1ms→2931.0ms using only 317 total discovery-agent calls versus 550 for Fixed Exploration; with Gemini-3.7-Flash, 2516.7ms→2350.6ms using 1879 versus 3200 calls. Both Dream-RSI variants beat sklearn and glmnet on all six held-out datasets and beat SimpleTES (51,200 generations with GPT-OSS-120B) on average downstream runtime while using roughly two orders of magnitude fewer discovery-agent calls. A qualitative analysis of the discovered solver (Appendix C) finds it introduces adaptive verification within active-set optimization — combining strong-rule screening with Cauchy-Schwarz-based KKT pruning that selectively recomputes exact gradients only when a bound cannot certify a feature — distinct from SimpleTES's dimension-triggered LARS/coordinate-descent switch.

**Mathematical optimization (Sec. 4.2, Table 1).** Sum-Difference, Circle Packing, and Autocorrelation Inequalities, 10 rounds with Gemini-3.1 Pro, compared against AlphaEvolve, AlphaEvolveV2, OpenEvolve, CodeEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, TTS-Discovery, ThetaEvolve, EvoX, and SimpleTES. Dream-RSI reaches 1.145427 on Sum-Diff (best among compared methods, versus 1.144047 for Recursive Fixed Exploration and 1.143975 for SimpleTES), 2.635983 on Circle Packing (matching the strongest reported result), and 1.456375 on Autocorrelation (competitive but not state-of-the-art — SimpleTES reaches 1.453675 using 51,200 generations versus Dream-RSI's under-1,000).

**GPU kernel engineering (Sec. 4.3, Fig. 4).** Four KernelBench tasks (VGG16, LayerNorm, ConvDiv, ConvMax) with Gemini-3.1 Pro as the coding agent, scored by inverse runtime (1/ms) subject to correctness against a reference implementation. Dream-RSI reaches comparable final performance to Recursive Fixed Exploration using 2.43× fewer generations on VGG16 and 1.79× fewer on LayerNorm; under matched generation budgets it reaches 2.09× higher performance on ConvDiv and 1.44× higher on ConvMax.

**Further analysis (Sec. 5).** (1) On ConvDiv, replacing interactive replay with prompt-level semantic guidance (abstracting prior trajectories into directional hints injected into the prompt) *underperforms* the unguided variant for both Dream-RSI and Fixed Exploration under equal budget — the paper interprets this as evidence that strong semantic priors over-constrain parallel exploration in long-horizon discovery, so the replay simulator's value comes from enabling cheap trial-and-error over concrete policy code, not merely from summarizing history into guidance text. (2) Tracking exploration behavior across 9 recursive rounds on ConvDiv shows the learned policy adaptively contracts effort when performance is improving (evaluated attempts dropping from 110 to 50 across early rounds) and re-expands effort when progress plateaus, coinciding with renewed gains.

# Q9 Contributions

1. Reconceptualizing a completed agent discovery history as a replay simulator: because every node's execution outcome is already recorded in the discovery tree, alternative exploration policies can be evaluated against it at zero additional execution cost, making previously delayed and expensive meta-level exploration feedback immediately and cheaply available.
2. Dream-RSI, a meta-layer recursive self-improvement loop (online exploration → replay-simulator construction → dreaming-based policy improvement → redeployment) that closes the loop between accumulated discovery experience and exploration-policy quality, with a selection rule (candidate set always includes the current policy) guaranteeing the redeployed policy is no worse than the previous one on average replay score over the fixed history.
3. Empirical validation across three distinct discovery domains (algorithm engineering, mathematical optimization, GPU kernel engineering) that dreaming-based policy improvement matches or exceeds a fixed-exploration-policy baseline's discovery quality while cutting discovery-agent calls substantially — up to roughly two orders of magnitude fewer calls than a reported non-fixed-policy baseline (SimpleTES) on the Lasso task, and 1.79×-2.43× fewer generations for comparable kernel performance on two of four KernelBench tasks.
4. A controlled ablation (semantic-guidance injection vs. interactive replay, Sec. 5.1) showing that the value of accumulated history for long-horizon discovery comes specifically from enabling cheap trial-and-error over many concrete policy variants, not merely from summarizing history into directional prompt guidance, which the paper finds actively over-constrains exploration under equal budget.
5. A qualitative account (Sec. 5.2) of how the learned exploration policy adapts its own effort level across recursive rounds — contracting evaluated-attempt counts as performance improves, then re-expanding when progress plateaus — offered as evidence the dreaming loop discovers genuinely adaptive, not merely more aggressive, exploration behavior.

# Q10 Next Steps

The paper's own Conclusion frames Dream-RSI as addressing "the core bottleneck of meta-optimization: delayed and expensive feedback," and its Related Work section explicitly separates this contribution from object-level self-evolution work, but the paper does not enumerate a dedicated Limitations section in the retrieved text; the closest such content is implicit in its own reported scope: all evaluated domains use a coding agent operating over well-defined, cheaply-scoreable numerical or code-correctness objectives (regression-path runtime, mathematical inequality values, kernel execution speed), and the replay-simulator mechanism's applicability to domains with less crisply verifiable outcomes (e.g., open-ended research ideation, or tasks whose evaluator itself is noisy or expensive) is not tested here.

Beyond the paper's own framing, three points are worth flagging for a reader assessing this note's evidence. First, replay evaluates a candidate policy only against outcomes *already recorded* in the accumulated history — a policy that would have explored a genuinely novel branch never taken by any prior online rollout cannot be credited for what it might have discovered there, since replay can only reveal recorded children; this means the replay objective structurally favors policies that reorganize known-good exploration patterns over policies whose main value would be exploring previously-unvisited regions of the search space, a limitation the paper's own formalism implies (Section 3's replay transition definition) but does not discuss as a limitation. Second, the paper's efficiency comparisons against external baselines (SimpleTES's "two orders of magnitude fewer calls," several math-optimization baselines) mix different underlying discovery-agent models and are not re-run under a shared harness, so as flagged in Q8, the magnitude of these specific cross-system efficiency claims should be read as directional rather than a strictly controlled measurement. Third, both ablation studies in Section 5 are conducted on a single task (ConvDiv); given the paper's own four-KernelBench-task main results already show uneven gains across tasks (2.43× vs. 1.44× fewer/more depending on task), whether the semantic-guidance and adaptive-effort findings generalize to the paper's other seven evaluated tasks — including the three math-optimization tasks and three other domains entirely — is an open empirical question this note's source material does not resolve.
