# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** an agent-evolution setup with frozen model weights: a proposer that writes candidate artifacts (prompts, skills, memory playbooks, harness code), an evaluation that scores the resulting agent on a task set, and a selector that keeps higher-scoring candidates.
* **Output (Y):** a diagnosis of where such loops fail, expressed through two measured variances, evaluation noise σ² (re-evaluating one artifact) and run-to-run variance τ² (true quality of artifacts from independent evolution runs), and through the *resolution* of an evaluation pipeline, the smallest true improvement it can reliably tell from noise.
* **Constraints:** every role of a method uses the same model; official implementations and equal budgets per model; evolution sees only training and validation splits; every in-loop validation score is one rollout per task.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The observation that evolving agents are fragile is not new: the paper cites work on sensitivity to run variance and task order, sparse feedback-dependent gains, skill-induced failures, contamination from uncontrolled skill accumulation, and PACE's analysis of false commits under repeated noisy comparisons. What is new is the isolation of one mechanism at two levels. The paper separates evaluation noise from run-to-run variance with a random-effects estimator, and uses frozen candidate pools to test proposal quality and selection reliability independently of each other, at a scale (8 to 16 evolution runs per configuration, each artifact evaluated 8 to 16 times) that single-run papers do not reach.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

**The Resolution Hypothesis:** the bottleneck of agent evolution is identifying which artifacts bring improvement, not proposing them. If this holds, then (P1) candidate pools contain artifacts that truly help, (P2) common selection mechanisms pick the better of two candidates at about chance, and (P3) per-round selection errors compound across rounds into the run-to-run variance that flips method rankings. At the method level it predicts that evolution accumulates only improvements larger than the resolution, so large reported gains should come from single behaviors that lift most tasks at once.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Evolution methods studied.** Text artifacts: GEPA (prompts), SkillOpt-Lite and Trace2Skill (skills), ReasoningBank (memory playbooks). Harness: HarnessLens (instructions, skills, tool descriptions), Meta-Harness and AHE (code).
* **Analyses of why evolving fails.** Run-variance and task-order sensitivity, matched-budget baselines, sparse gains across rounds, SkillsBench and follow-up studies of skill-induced failure and contamination, SEAGym and continual-learning evaluations.
* **Statistics of noisy selection.** Best-arm identification, overfitting of model-selection criteria, uncertainty-aware RL evaluation, PACE's anytime-valid acceptance test.
* **Outside the scope.** Program evolution with deterministic scoring (GPU kernel optimization, ARC-AGI-2, Atari policy search), which the paper names as structurally lower-noise and untested here.
* **Key people.** Yuanhao Zeng and Kan Ren (ShanghaiTech University; the two contact emails). Likely critics: authors of the audited methods, who could object that the one-rule test is chosen after inspecting artifacts and that the candidate-level evidence comes from 10 to 11 Terminal-Bench tasks.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Variance decomposition.** Var(Ŷ) = σ² + τ². Judging one fixed artifact against a reference is limited by σ² alone; comparing two methods is limited by τ² + σ², because each run draws a new artifact. Runs needed to detect a margin Δ at 80% power and α = 0.05: N ≥ 2((z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)/Δ)²(τ² + σ²).
2. **Gain audit.** For the largest reported gains, find by instruction-level inspection (LLM flagging plus manual check) the one rule most responsible, append only that rule to the unevolved baseline, then repair the evaluation (restore the official grader or grade by meaning) and re-score; on four benchmarks, re-evolve under the repaired evaluation.
3. **Frozen candidate pools.** A Proposal Pool (100 skills from a SkillOpt-Lite-based proposer on 10 Terminal-Bench v2.1 training tasks, scored on 10 disjoint test tasks, 16 rollouts per skill-task pair) measures Quality@K, the expected success of the best of K random skills, against an equal-quality null that removes the upward bias of taking a maximum over noisy scores. A Selection Pool (100 skills from Terminal-Bench and DeepSWE trajectories, 11 difficulty-stratified tasks, 8 to 15 rollouts per pair) lets each selector see the first one or two rollouts and scores its choice on the held-out rest.
4. **Trajectory re-evaluation.** Every intermediate artifact of eight SkillOpt-Lite and eight GEPA runs is re-scored with eight rollouts per task where evolution used one.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Models.** GPT-5.4 nano, GPT-5.6 Luna and Terra, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Kimi-K3, GLM-5.2; cheaper experiments use all six, expensive ones a subset.
* **Variance grid.** Seven methods on ALFWorld, SpreadsheetBench, OfficeQA, and Terminal-Bench v2.1: 150 configurations (117 method configurations plus baselines), 8 evolution runs each (16 for text-artifact methods on GPT-5.4 nano), each artifact evaluated 16 times (8 on Terminal-Bench and for harness methods).
* **Literature survey.** 171 benchmark-model results from the main tables of nine agent-evolution papers.
* **Gain audit.** Twelve benchmarks (three from the grid plus HotpotQA, PUPA, HoVer, IFBench, AIME 2025, LiveBench-Math, LiveMathematicianBench, SearchQA, DocVQA) with three models.
* **Selectors compared.** Random, single-rollout execution score, the native SkillOpt-Lite and GEPA parent selection (two rollouts), and three LLM trajectory judges (pointwise, pairwise, ACE-style) with DeepSeek-V4-Flash as judge.
* **Total cost stated.** Over 10M LLM calls and 400K CPU core hours.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

**Method level.**

* Evaluation noise σ is 1.0 to 3.7 pp on SpreadsheetBench, OfficeQA, and ALFWorld and reaches 4.5 pp on Terminal-Bench; run-to-run τ ranges from 0 to 12.3 pp. A method evolved once and evaluated once has a typical standard deviation of 1.7 to 4.1 pp (median per benchmark).
* For over 60% of same-model, same-benchmark configuration pairs, the gap between all-run means is below one standard deviation of a single-run comparison (53% to 79% per method).
* In the surveyed papers the proposed method leads its strongest competitor by a median 3.4 pp (interquartile range 1.2 to 6.6); none of the 171 results reports a variance estimate and 6 average over more than one evolution run.
* Detecting 3.4 pp needs about 5 runs per method on SpreadsheetBench, 10 on OfficeQA, 19 on ALFWorld, and 23 on Terminal-Bench (181 at the lower-quartile margin). A one-run-per-method table picks the truly best of four text-artifact methods 51% to 73% of the time on the first three benchmarks and 33% on Terminal-Bench, against 25% for guessing.

**Gain audit.**

* Across the 27 model-benchmark pairs with a gain above 3 pp, one appended rule recovers a median 90% of the gain. Examples: on SpreadsheetBench the released grader reads formula cells without recalculation, so "write computed values, never formula strings" is worth about 30 pp; on LiveMathematicianBench a "stronger result" option is correct whenever it appears.
* After repair the median gain falls from 17.6 to 0.8 pp. Re-evolved text-artifact methods gain at most 1.9 pp on OfficeQA and SpreadsheetBench, with one exception (+5.8 pp). HotpotQA is a stated exception where GEPA improves retrieval under either evaluation, and no shortcut was found on ALFWorld, PUPA, HoVer, or IFBench.
* Harness methods re-evolved under repaired evaluations gain at most 9 pp on SpreadsheetBench and OfficeQA; the larger ALFWorld gains (16 to 32 pp for Meta-Harness and AHE) trace to one setting, an initial agent that keeps only its last two history steps.

**Candidate level (Terminal-Bench only).**

* P1: Quality@K rises from 49.2% at K = 1 to 61.8% at K = 100 under a 2-hour budget (39.7% to 51.6% under the original timeouts), 3.3 to 3.4 pp above the equal-quality null at K = 100. The best single shared skill reaches 57.5% against a 49.2% pool mean and 41.9% for the weakest.
* P2 (Table 1, posterior mean ± 95% half-width):

| Selection | Pairwise acc. (%) | False accept. (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 50.0 ± 0.0 | 30.5 ± 14.0 |
| Execution (1 rollout) | 49.7 ± 1.9 | 62.1 ± 42.9 |
| SkillOpt-Lite (2 rollouts) | 48.6 ± 2.6 | 59.4 ± 46.2 |
| GEPA (2 rollouts) | 48.6 ± 2.6 | 31.6 ± 14.5 |
| LLM judge (pointwise) | 45.4 ± 4.5 | 39.9 ± 17.5 |
| LLM judge (pairwise) | 49.0 ± 2.2 | 57.6 ± 23.3 |
| LLM judge (ACE) | 46.9 ± 3.5 | 39.0 ± 15.6 |

  The rank correlation between one execution score and posterior quality is 0.054. No selector improves as K grows to 100, while an oracle passes them all within K < 5.
* P3: in-loop validation gains grow to 6.9 to 9.0 pp over evolution while re-evaluated gains stay between +0.4 and −3.2 pp; corrected run-to-run variance grows from 0 to 4.2 to 24.5 squared pp by the final round.
* A direct intervention (Appendix F.3): averaging 16 validation rollouts per candidate in SkillOpt-Lite raises the mean final test score over 16 runs from 61.4% to 64.4% and the best artifact from 64.8% to 72.7%.

**Limits on the evidence.**

* The candidate-level results rest on one benchmark, 10 and 11 tasks, 100 skills per pool, one proposer family, and (for P3) two methods and two models; the paper lists this itself.
* P2 is a statement about selection from one or two rollouts, the budget the methods actually use. It does not show that selection is impossible, and the 16-rollout experiment shows it recovers with more evaluation (reader framing of results the paper reports).
* The false-acceptance column has intervals as wide as ±42.9 points; the paper says they leave open whether execution-based selection is worse than random acceptance. The pairwise-accuracy column is the tight one.
* The one-rule test picks the rule after inspecting the artifacts, so it shows one sentence is sufficient for the gain, not that the artifacts contain nothing else (stated in Appendix E.1). An LLM agent did the first-pass flagging.
* The audit covers GEPA and SkillOpt-Lite released artifacts plus the authors' own runs; released artifacts are transferred to different models than they were optimized on, which the paper notes typically shrinks the gain.
* Nothing is released yet, so none of this can be re-run today.

# Q9 Contributions

1. A stated, testable hypothesis that frozen-weight agent evolution is limited by identification, with a variance decomposition that says how many runs a claimed margin needs.
2. The largest reproducibility grid for agent evolution reported so far (by the authors' account), showing published margins are within single-run noise on four benchmarks.
3. An audit that finds grader or port shortcuts in the released evaluations of eight of twelve benchmarks and shows the large gains there mostly vanish when the evaluation is repaired.
4. Controlled pool experiments on Terminal-Bench separating proposal from selection, plus two proposed diagnostics (Quality@K for proposers, selected-quality curves for selectors) and a call to report σ̂ and τ̂.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Stated by the paper.** Lower-variance intermediate checks (unmasked-diagnostic check rates show 13× to 57× lower evaluation noise than pass/fail verdicts in a preliminary analysis, though triggering more checks need not solve more tasks); component-wise evaluation of proposers and selectors; testing whether resolution constrains program evolution and RL-based weight updates.
* **Budget allocation.** With a fixed rollout budget, how should an evolution loop split it between more candidates and more rollouts per candidate? The 16-rollout result and PACE-style sequential tests suggest racing or successive-halving selectors as the obvious next experiment (reader suggestion).
* **Paired evaluation.** Table 1's selectors compare unpaired noisy scores. Common random seeds, cached environment states, or per-task paired differences might raise resolution at no extra rollout cost (reader suggestion; not tested in the paper).
* **For this repository.** Any skill or harness self-improvement loop in a research OS that accepts edits on one validation pass is subject to the same failure; logging repeated evaluations of the incumbent gives a local σ̂ before trusting an accepted edit (reader inference).
