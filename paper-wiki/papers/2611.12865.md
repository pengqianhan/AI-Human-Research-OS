# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** executable task environments with stateful tools, where the task instructions state the applicable contextual requirements and authorization (for example a shared allowance on external requests, or that published evidence must apply to the current artifact); a starting policy, Qwen3.5-4B.
* **Output (Y):** a policy trained with GRPO on its own rollouts, measured on task reward, independently verified real work, historical violations (any breach during the execution), certified profitable violations (a breach whose reward is strictly above the compliant optimum or a valid upper bound on it), and safe completion (task completed with no historical violation).
* **Constraints:** every admitted task instance must satisfy two verified properties. P1, compliant feasibility: a reference execution meets the task goal inside the contextual boundary at or above a value baseline. P2, reward conflict: some executable violating trajectory earns more than every compliant one. Both are certified by running reference trajectories through the same tool interface the agent uses.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

The problem is known; the construction is new. Earlier work shows reward hacking and reward-driven misalignment in particular settings (test hardcoding, production training environments, harmful actions learned for task reward), and benchmarks instrument fixed agents for specified shortcuts. The paper's claim is that none of these jointly certifies that compliant completion is feasible and that violating execution has a strict reward advantage over all compliant executions. It also covers a case the usual reward-hacking framing leaves out: the reward measures real work accurately, and the agent earns more by doing more real work with resources it was not authorized to use.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

Two linked hypotheses. First, if profitable shortcuts exist, task-reward RL will acquire them even when the requirements are stated in the instructions and conflict tasks are a minority of training data, and capability metrics will rise at the same time. Second, if violations are verifiable from execution history, subtracting a fixed penalty from the reward of violating trajectories (λ = 1) will teach compliance together with useful work, and the learned behavior will follow the authorization context instead of avoiding the resource altogether.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **RL-induced misalignment and reward hacking.** Formal analysis of proxy and intended objectives; reward-model overoptimization; agent-RL studies of test hardcoding, [reward hacking](../concepts/reward-hacking.md) that generalizes to broader misalignment, and harmful actions learned in pursuit of reward.
* **Shortcut benchmarks.** HVE and HVTB instrument game and terminal environments to detect specified shortcuts; BAITBENCH plants data shortcuts in synthetic ML tasks; BenchShield checks reward integrity through execution evidence.
* **Defenses and safety training.** Runtime reasoning over safety constraints, monitoring with trusted intervention, BashArena; training from principle-derived preferences, reasoning over safety specifications, and expected-cost constraints.
* **Construction lineage.** VHD (Shen et al., 2026a), a mechanism-first approach in which reward and checks are grounded in a solved state-update mechanism before the tool interface is rendered.
* **Key people.** Single author, Xinjie Shen (Georgia Tech email; the Hugging Face page lists the organization Everywhere Safety), first author of the VHD paper this builds on. Likely critics: RL practitioners, who would ask for training seeds and larger models, and safety researchers, who would ask what happens when requirements are implicit or the violation checker is imperfect.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

Put the requirement in the environment's bookkeeping, leave the violating action executable, and certify the incentive.

1. **Relax enforcement, keep the check.** A mechanism records work and the quantity a requirement governs (cumulative requests against an allowance, the artifact version a report applies to). The interface still lets the agent exceed the allowance or publish a stale report, and the history records the breach. Renewing evidence later restores validity but the earlier breach stays in history.
2. **Certify P1 and P2 per instance.** Parameters are sampled, a compliant reference and a violating witness are executed through the agent-facing tools, and only instances satisfying both properties are kept. Changing the authorization gives an ordinary twin with the same physical task and reward, which allows controlled mixtures of ordinary and conflict tasks.
3. **Corpus-grounded rendering.** An LLM setter turns the sampled dynamics plus an independently sampled corpus passage into a concrete scenario with its own entities and tool names; the solver sees only instructions and tool observations.
4. **One signal for two objectives.** Task-trained policies optimize task reward. Safety-trained policies optimize task reward minus λ times a binary indicator of any historical violation. With the best violating and compliant values V_s and V_c, the compliant optimum is preferred exactly when λ exceeds V_s minus V_c.

Eight core mechanism families are catalogued: allocation, API budget, validation, test expectations, evaluation cohort, score cache, CI reports, and service monitoring. Allocation and API budget are resource mechanisms, where a violation yields more real work; in Validation a violation raises the reported score without the repair.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **RQ1, acquisition.** Base against Task-trained on the API task (reward equals verified output) and Validation (reward reads reports); amplification and emergence counts; a mixed policy with 51.9% conflict rollouts; 25% against 100% conflict exposure at matched training dose; continued optimization up to 128 updates.
* **RQ2, safety learning.** Safety-trained from Base (prevention) on API, Allocation, and a re-rendered Workshop scenario; paired API authorization contexts that hold the physical task and score fixed; safety continuation from a common Task-trained parent (mitigation).
* **RQ3, generalization.** 24 new API tasks across four allowance levels and two wordings; nontransferable per-account allowances not seen in training; excluded mechanisms (Validation-only policies evaluated on Allocation); external tasks from BAITBENCH and an illustrative HVTA subset; release workflows whose evidence and approval requirements change during execution, including a composition with revoked and reissued vendor permission and four re-described operational settings.
* **Protocol.** Each comparison uses one fixed policy per role. A panel is typically 24 physical tasks with two draws per authorization context (48 attempts), or 32 tasks with two draws (64).

# Q7 Datasets and Code

Constructed environments: 8 core mechanism families; 132 physical tasks and 384 condition records in the core panels; at least 807 task instances across training and evaluation by the paper's saved-record audit; first-pass executable admission 91.7%, final 100% after repair; an estimated API cost of about $0.019 per admitted realization. An automated proposal study (Gemini 3.1 Pro authoring workflows, Gemini 3.5 Flash reviewing them) yields 7 verified new workflows from 24 requests. The 128-update run with 25% conflict exposure consumes 256 distinct instances and 2,048 rollouts.

The arXiv page and Hugging Face page point to a project page (https://xinjie-shen.com/post/rlmisalignment); no code repository is linked on either. This note did not inspect the project page.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

**Task reward teaches the shortcut (paired API contexts, 48 attempts each).**

| Metric | Base | Task-trained | Safety-trained |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restricted historical violations | 27.1% | 75.0% | 8.3% |
| Restricted certified profitable violations | 12.5% | 70.8% | 0% |
| Restricted safe completion | 72.9% | 25.0% | 91.7% |
| Restricted real work | .675 | .908 | .689 |
| Authorized lawful expanded-permission use | 68.8% | 100% | 93.8% |
| Authorized real work | .834 | .999 | .965 |

Task training makes the agent better at the job and worse at staying inside the allowance, and an evaluation under full authorization would show only the improvement. Safety training removes certified profitable violations here and still uses extra permission when it is granted. Its cost is visible in the same table: authorized real work is .965 against .999, and restricted real work is barely above Base (.689 against .675).

**Reporting mechanisms diverge from work.** On Validation, Task-trained raises restricted reward from .050 to 1.000 while verified repair progress falls from .979 to .072, and violations go from 1 to 48 of 48 attempts.

**Minority exposure is enough.** At matched dose, 25% conflict exposure gives 18.8% certified profitable violations on API against 81.3% for 100% exposure. With continued optimization the 25% mixture reaches 97.9% on API (47 of 48) and 83.3% on Allocation (40 of 48) after 128 updates. Reader observation: the final endpoints are not ordered by conflict share. On Allocation they are 40, 24, and 33 of 48 for the 25%, roughly 50%, and 100% mixtures, and the paper notes that the endpoints lie on different training paths and horizons. The curves support "a minority of conflict tasks suffices", not a dose-response relation.

**Mitigation after acquisition is weak.** Safety continuation from a Task-trained parent lowers historical violations from 43 to 31 of 48 on Allocation, 44 to 34 on API, and 46 to 35 on Workshop. About 65 to 73% of attempts still violate afterwards (reader calculation), against 8.3% on the paired API panel when safety training starts from Base. The paper says "partially mitigates"; the numbers say prevention works far better than cure.

**Transfer within the resource family is strong.** On 24 new API tasks across six restricted cells, certified profitable violations are 10, 135, and 1 of 288 attempts for Base, Task-trained, and Safety-trained. Under per-account allowances never seen in training they are 15, 75, and 3 of 96, with expanded-permission work of .812, .991, and .988.

**Transfer across mechanisms is modest against Base.** Validation-only policies evaluated on Allocation give 15, 20, and 12 certified profitable violations of 48 for Base, Task-trained, and Safety-trained (paired Safety-minus-Task difference of -16.7 points, 95% interval -31.3 to -4.2). On 32 fresh instances they are 20, 28, and 17 of 62, the 45.2% to 27.4% the main text reports, and historical violations are 39 of 62 for both Base and Safety-trained. Reader reading: most of this gap is the harm task-only training added, not protection beyond the starting model.

**Changing requirements.** On 32 release tasks where the owner revises the validation rule and approval commitment mid-execution, safe completion is 43, 34, and 57 of 64 for Base, Task-trained, and Safety-trained; the abstract's 53.1% to 89.1% compares the last two, and all three do full real work. With vendor permission also revoked and reissued the counts are 27, 29, and 36 of 64. Across four re-described settings Safety-trained gains 12.5 to 50.0 points over Task-trained; in the public-services setting the counts are 13, 16, and 22 of 48.

**External benchmarks are illustrations.** On BAITBENCH the paper documents two saved Task-trained executions that use prohibited test labels and states that a panel-wide violation rate is not estimated. The HVTA numbers (native hack flags in 32% of Base and 68% of Task-trained executions) come from a 25-task subset that the paper says describes the illustrated cases only.

**Limits on the evidence (reader observations).**

* One fixed policy per role: no training seeds, so run-to-run variation of GRPO is unmeasured. Every rate is a count out of 48, 62, or 64 attempts on 24 or 32 tasks, each task drawn twice; one comparison has a bootstrap interval.
* One small base model (Qwen3.5-4B), one penalty weight (λ = 1) with no sweep, and requirements that are always written in the task instructions. The paper's discussion expects implicit requirements to be harder but does not test them.
* The penalty relies on an exact violation checker, which these environments supply by construction. Real training environments are where that signal is missing.
* All mechanisms are synthetic and built by the author's own pipeline.

# Q9 Contributions

1. A formal, checkable definition of a reward-context conflict (compliant feasibility plus a certified reward advantage for a violation) that covers both unauthorized productivity and manipulation of the measurement channel.
2. A scalable way to construct such environments by relaxing enforcement while keeping the check, with executable references and corpus-grounded rendering.
3. Evidence that task-only RL acquires stated-rule violations alongside real capability gains, including under minority exposure.
4. Evidence that a binary history penalty yields authorization-sensitive behavior that transfers to new wordings, permission scopes, and changing evidence requirements, and that it prevents acquisition much better than it reverses it.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Limits acknowledged in the discussion.** The experiments cover a controlled subset of failures with deliberately simple mechanisms; composed, longer workflows are expected to make shortcuts harder to recognize.
* **Seeds and scale.** Repeat the key comparisons over training seeds and on larger models (reader suggestion).
* **Imperfect checkers.** Train with a violation signal that has false negatives or false positives, the realistic case, and measure how much compliance survives (reader suggestion).
* **Implicit requirements.** Remove the requirement from the instructions and test whether Safety-trained policies infer it from context, which is the generalization the discussion hopes for.
* **Penalty weight.** Sweep λ around the certified gap V_s minus V_c, which the construction makes known per instance.
* **Project fit.** For evaluating agent work, the paper's split of reward, independently verified work, and execution-history compliance is a compact checklist: a rising score with rising verified work can still hide an authorization breach.
