# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a long-horizon agentic task (a DeepSWE v1.1 or Terminal-Bench 2.1 task), a capable but expensive model and an efficient, cheaper model, both running in the same mini-SWE-agent scaffold, and the growing trajectory of whichever model is acting.
* **Output (Y):** a decision after each turn on whether to hand the task to the other model, plus the transfer of the workspace and the history; the receiving model then finishes the task alone. The evaluated outcome is task accuracy and mean API cost per task.
* **Constraints:** one handoff per task, never switching back; the receiving model inherits all file changes and gets the history either in its context or as a JSONL log file it can inspect; cost counts only cloud API calls, and locally deployable models are charged zero.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. Query-level routing and cascades (FrugalGPT, RouteLLM, RouterDC, RouterBench) choose one model per request, and multi-round routers (Router-R1, ToolOrchestra, SkillOrchestra, MTRouter, Agent-as-a-Router) choose a model or tool per step. Closest are SWE-Router (an exploratory trajectory informs escalation), TACIT-Switch (permanent small-to-large handoffs from risk signals), and The Handoff Tax (arXiv:2608.24358), which already studied handoff direction, timing, and transfer interfaces at predefined switch points. AgentBaton's contribution is the finding that the profitable direction is capable-to-efficient and a learned detector that decides the switch time online for any model pair.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If a capable model starts a long-horizon task and hands off once it has produced a substantive artifact, obtained task-relevant feedback on it, and narrowed the remaining work to local repair or validation, then a much cheaper model can finish the task with little accuracy loss; the opposite direction, letting a cheap model start and escalating later, will not save much because the capable model must recover from the cheap model's errors. A small learned detector should recognize the readiness signs better than prompting the capable model to hand off or switching at a fixed turn.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Request-level routing and cascades.** FrugalGPT, Dekoninck et al.'s unified routing and cascading, RouteLLM, RouterDC, Route to Reason, and RouterBench.
* **Multi-round routing and orchestration.** Router-R1, ToolOrchestra (arXiv:2511.21689, from overlapping NVIDIA authors), SkillOrchestra, MTRouter (turn-level routing from history and model embeddings, reproduced as a baseline), and Agent-as-a-Router.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

**Analysis first.** Run each model alone, replay a recorded trajectory up to a checkpoint (15% to 90% of the source model's completed trajectory) in a fresh container, and let the other model continue. Compare both directions and both context-transfer methods. Three capable models (GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3, at max reasoning effort) and one efficient model (Qwen3.8-27B at xhigh, run locally) are used.

**What makes a good handoff point.** From trajectories where Qwen fails after one checkpoint but succeeds after the next, the authors name three readiness signs: (1) a substantive implementation, runnable prototype, or required artifact exists; (2) executing, testing, or inspecting it has given task-relevant feedback, which may be a failure if it narrows the problem; (3) the remaining work is localized diagnosis, repair, validation, or final output. Figure 4's examples include a quoted-path bug fixed before handoff and a coordinate transform validated before handoff.

**AgentBaton.** A Qwen3.5-2B model reads the task and the capable model's actions and observations after every turn and outputs `HANDOFF_READY` or `HANDOFF_NOT_READY`; the first ready decision transfers control. Training data are 4,000 mini-SWE-agent trajectories on 2,000 SWE-rebench V2 tasks from five capable models (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3), with Codex annotating the earliest turn that meets all three readiness signs (or "no clear handoff point"), seeing only tool calls and outputs, never final rewards. Every turn after the labelled point is also a positive example, so the detector can still fire after missing the first chance. Training takes two epochs at 32K context on 16 H100s; a 9B variant brought no clear gain. Median detection latency is 338 ms (p95 1.11 s) on one H100.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Benchmarks.** [DeepSWE](../concepts/deepswe.md) v1.1 (113 long-horizon software-engineering tasks with per-task verifiers) and [Terminal-Bench](../concepts/terminal-bench.md) 2.1, both under [mini-SWE-agent](../concepts/mini-swe-agent.md) v2.4.0 with a two-hour limit per task and 16K new tokens per turn.
* **Direction and transfer (Table 1).** Both directions at 30% and 75% checkpoints, in-context against logfile transfer, for three capable models paired with Qwen3.8-27B.
* **Timing curves (Figure 3, Figure 6).** Six checkpoints from 15% to 90% in both directions.
* **Main results (Table 3).** Five capable models (adding Claude Opus 5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash) handing off to Qwen3.8-27B or to GPT-5.6 Luna, plus Luna handing off to Qwen: 11 pairs × 2 benchmarks = 22 settings. Complete results for both transfer methods are in Table 5.
* **Baselines (Table 2, Table 4).** Prompting the capable model to hand off itself; a query-level router (Qwen3.5-2B trained to predict whether Qwen3.8-27B solves a task); a reproduced MTRouter; and fixed-progress and fixed-turn handoffs set to AgentBaton's own mean handoff point.
* **Effort sweep (Figure 1, Table 6).** Source models at several reasoning efforts on DeepSWE, each with AgentBaton handoff to Qwen and, for four sources, to Luna.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * *Direction matters.* Efficient-to-capable handoffs mostly lose accuracy or save little; the best case, Qwen to GPT-5.6 Sol at 30%, reaches 70.80% on DeepSWE against 68.10% for Sol alone, but cuts cost by only 6.1%. Capable-to-efficient handoffs can keep accuracy at a fraction of the cost: Kimi K3 to Qwen at 45% gives 72.57% against 73.45% at 61.5% lower cost.
  * *Large savings across pairs.* The 22 relative cost reductions in Table 3 average 65.9% (reader check of the abstract's figure), and 12 of the 22 settings gain accuracy or lose less than 5% relative.
  * *A stronger receiver helps.* With GPT-5.6 Luna as receiver, four of five capable models exceed their own DeepSWE accuracy at 66.6% to 73.9% lower cost, for example Opus 5 to Luna at 76.1% against 67.3%.
  * *Prompting does not replace the detector.* Asked to hand off when ready, Kimi K3 never does (0 of 113 tasks) and GLM-5.3 does on 6 of 113; GPT-5.6 Sol always does but still costs $3.75 per task against $5.32 alone.
  * *Online detection beats simple rules.* Against MTRouter, AgentBaton costs 46.7% to 86.6% less with equal or higher accuracy in all four settings tested; it beats fixed-turn handoff on accuracy and matches fixed-progress handoff, which uses the source model's future trajectory length, at equal or lower cost.
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * *The cheapest model is free by convention.* Qwen3.8-27B costs $0 because it can run on local hardware, and the detector's GPU time is not charged either. Savings with Qwen as receiver are therefore savings in API spend, not in total compute or hardware (reader observation); the Luna-receiver results, where both sides are paid APIs, are the cleaner cost test.
  * *Single runs on small task sets.* No repeated runs or intervals are reported. One DeepSWE task is 0.88 points, and every Terminal-Bench 2.1 value fits a set of 89 tasks, so one task is about 1.1 points there (reader inference from the values). Many "preserved accuracy" claims are differences of one to three tasks.
  * *The same configuration appears with different numbers.* Table 3 gives GPT-5.6 Sol at max effort 68.1% and $5.32 on DeepSWE, and GLM-5.3 61.1% and $3.16, while Table 6, described as the same DeepSWE sweep, gives 71.7% and $4.84 and 69.0% and $3.06; AgentBaton GLM-5.3 to Qwen is 58.4% in Table 3 and 66.4% in Table 6. The Kimi K3 and Opus 5 rows match. The text does not explain the difference, which suggests run-to-run variation of up to about 8 points (9 tasks) for one configuration (reader observation).
  * *The accuracy-gain headline uses a weak baseline.* The "up to 8.8 points" is Opus 5 to Luna (76.1%) against Opus 5 at max effort (67.3%); in Table 6, Opus 5 at xhigh effort alone scores 75.2%, so the gain over the best Opus configuration is about 1 point, at much lower cost (reader observation).
  * *Two benchmarks, one scaffold, coding only.* Both benchmarks are code or terminal tasks under mini-SWE-agent, and detector training uses SWE-rebench V2, the same family of work; four of the five training models are also capable models at test time. Gemini 3.8 Flash and GPT-5.6 Luna are the only unseen sources.
  * *Labels come from one model.* Codex annotates handoff points without a human-agreement check.
  * *Two transfer methods, chosen per source.* The transfer method for each source model was fixed after the Section 3 comparison on the same benchmarks; Table 5 shows that the other method can be several points worse (Kimi K3 to Qwen on DeepSWE: 72.6% in context, 65.5% by logfile).

# Q9 Contributions

* A controlled analysis of one-time handoffs inside long-horizon agent tasks: direction, context transfer, and timing across model pairs and two benchmarks.
* Three observable readiness signs for handing off from a capable to a cheap model, written as an annotation protocol.
* AgentBaton, a small learned per-turn detector, with comparisons against prompting, query-level routing, a per-turn router, and fixed thresholds.
* Effort-sweep evidence that handoff and reasoning effort are complementary ways to buy accuracy, with configurations beyond the single-model accuracy-cost frontier on DeepSWE.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Repeated runs and total-cost accounting (reader suggestion).** Three runs per configuration and a cost model that charges local inference and detection would show which savings survive noise and hardware cost.
* **Beyond coding (reader suggestion).** Tool-agent-user tasks, web search, or GUI tasks may have different readiness signs; whether a detector trained on SWE trajectories transfers there is untested.
* **Handoff back up.** The paper argues per-turn switching accumulates errors, but a second, rare escalation when the cheap model stalls could recover some losses; the one-way design leaves this open.
* **Human-checked labels.** Agreement between Codex's annotated handoff points and human judgments would bound label noise.
* **Combine with harness choice (reader connection).** HEELO's finding that a strong model pays off most in diagnosis suggests splitting roles, not just turns, between a strong and a weak model.
