# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** completed Lean formalizations of named mathematical results, written by humans, AI, or both, scattered across independent repositories pinned to different Lean and Mathlib versions. Also the stream of new Lean/Mathlib releases.
* **Output (Y):** one continuously building archive where every admitted project compiles against the current shared Lean/Mathlib environment and passes kernel, linter, and admission checks. Each project keeps its attribution and provenance, and its results can be found, inspected, and imported by later work.
* **Core problem:** AI makes producing proofs cheap, so checking and *keeping reusable* becomes the bottleneck. Mathlib's strict human review makes it grow roughly linearly and it lacks much research-level material. Meanwhile standalone formalization repositories rot as Lean and Mathlib change underneath them. The question is whether AI agents, under mechanical checks and human oversight, can do the discovery, import, upgrade repair, optimization, and review work that keeps a large formal archive alive.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. Maintained formal archives exist: Isabelle's Archive of Formal Proofs (AFP) combines contributed formalizations with review and upkeep, and in Lean, Mathlib integrates everything into one API, Tau Ceti integrates contributions under stricter performance gates, and Palomar Registry registers separate repositories. What is new is the maintenance *model*: independent projects are kept as separate attributed developments but built together, and most recurring labour — finding eligible projects, importing them, repairing them after each upgrade, golfing proofs, reviewing statements — is assigned to agents. The paper is an operational record, not a controlled experiment. It matters to agent research as a rare long-running deployment where agent work is continuously checked by a hard verifier (the Lean kernel) plus a softer LLM review of statement faithfulness.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If an archive combines a hard correctness oracle (the Lean kernel), strict mechanical admission rules, LLM review of statement faithfulness, and scheduled agent jobs for discovery, repair, and optimization, then agent-assisted maintenance can (a) restore compatibility of hundreds of independently written projects after each dependency upgrade, (b) shrink and speed up the library without breaking it, and (c) produce an archive that later research formalization actually reuses. The paper supports these claims with its own operating record rather than with experiments designed to test them.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

Separate *correctness* (delegated entirely to the kernel and mechanical checks) from *upkeep labour* (delegated mostly to agents), and keep humans for admission decisions and oversight.

* **Admission rules.**
  * No `sorry` or `admit`, and no axioms beyond `Classical.choice`, `propext`, and `Quot.sound`.
  * No `set_option`, unchecked declarations, linter waivers, broad whole-Mathlib imports, or mechanisms that bypass resource limits.
  * An Apache-2.0 or MIT license.
  * A project card naming authors, upstream source, proof provenance (human, AI, or mixed), and main results paired with informal statements.
  * Open problems are kept on a separate challenge board, whose solutions are checked against the registered statements.
* **CI:** full-library build, warning checks, Mathlib's environment and style linters, archive-specific checks (cards, attribution, axioms, proof and file sizes, bypass attempts), and a compiled-environment audit. Profiling is advisory, not a merge gate (unlike Tau Ceti).
* **Scheduled agent jobs** (prompts plus a host runner in a separate automation repository): recent and older discovery → attributed import PRs; open-PR review and repair; project optimization; resolving maintainer issues; and announcements to the Lean Zulip channel.
* **Dependency upgrades** run as a separate workflow: detect a release, probe every project under the new versions, assign each failing project to a repair agent, assemble the patches, build the combined archive, and open a draft PR.
* **LLM mathematical review:** checks faithfulness, novelty, significance, sources, and code quality. The later version splits large changes into source portions and records which ranges were actually shown to the model.
* **Exposition site and discovery:** per-project dependency graphs linking each informal claim to its Lean statement, plus semantic search (Octo), so readers can find results and check their assumptions before importing them.

# Q6 Experiment Design

This is an observational operating record, not a controlled study. Evidence types and their scope:

* **Census (Table 1):** 211 completed projects, 7,043 files, 3,228,485 physical source lines, 193,862 declaration commands, 837 registered main results; provenance 70 human / 102 AI / 39 mixed; 18 commit contributors; 63 merged community PRs.
* **Upgrade replays (Table 2):** original pre-upgrade source is replayed against each new environment to count projects that fail to compile. The last row uses the retained production probe.
* **Optimization builds (Tables 3-4, 20):** for each accepted change, one clean before-and-after build of the whole library on the same Azure VM (16 CPUs, 125.8 GiB RAM), plus project-level timings taken from the PRs, on differing machines.
* **Review census (Figure 3, Tables 5, 22-23):** retained structured review reports, their verdicts, and later merge or close outcomes. Review accuracy was *not* independently labeled.
* **Reuse:** the external LeanEval audit (Figure 6) and a list of recorded source-level reuse (Table 24).
* **Build-resource comparison (Table 12):** one clean build each of Lean Pool, Mathlib, and Tau Ceti on the same VM.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported:**
  * *Compatibility can be restored at scale.* Upgrade breakage is large: 44 of 59, 58 of 91, 100 of 143, 19 of 145, 3 of 148, and 97 of 191 projects failed to compile on successive upgrades. On the stable-Lean migration, 95 of 97 repair jobs succeeded, and the accepted migration passed the combined build and all repository checks. Accepted upgrades changed 4,676-25,583 lines each.
  * *Library-wide shortening works.* Accepted changes removed up to 54,965 lines (elaboration-cost reduction, 5.8% faster build) and 45,217 lines (library-wide compression, 3.3% faster).
  * *Reuse happens.* Lean Pool is the most-matched external repository in the LeanEval structural audit.
* **Weaker than the headline suggests:**
  * *Shorter is not always faster.* Contributor proof golfing removed 12,515 lines yet made the clean build 5.4% *slower* (21.83 → 23.01 min), though it used less memory. Every before-and-after pair is a single run on a shared VM that was running background tasks.
  * *Repair was not fully autonomous.* The two failed repair jobs (graph fundamental groups, incompleteness) and remaining warnings and API breaks were fixed through further agent-assisted integration. That integration *strengthened auxiliary hypotheses* (measurability, sigma-finiteness, maximality). The paper notes that a successful build therefore does not prove every declaration is equivalent before and after — a quiet change to what a theorem states.
  * *LLM review evidence is thin.* The census counts verdicts and PR outcomes but not whether the reviews were correct. Only 69 same-PR report pairs exist and 37 agree (about 54%, reader-computed), with zero same-revision pairs. The service was manually disabled before the observation date. Recorded prices are $308.52 across 285 API-billed reports and $752.38 across only 6 Codex-quota reports (API-equivalent estimates, not invoices).
  * *Agent labour is not measured.* Job definitions list responsibilities, but the paper states they do not establish which model did which historical contribution. Human labour and monetary cost were not logged, so the human-versus-agent split of maintenance is unknown.
  * *Cost of the shared model.* The archive is larger and slower to build than Mathlib: 3.23 vs. 2.33 million source lines, 60.28 vs. 37.44 minutes, 20.03 vs. 7.35 GiB RAM.
* **Authorship disclosure:** the human-written portion is one page, and the author states the rest was produced almost entirely by AI. The AI-use statement says reported numbers are recomputed from retained source records, validated by source hashes, and regenerated tables. This note treats the figures as reported, not independently verified.

# Q9 Contributions

* A running, public archive that shows a *separate-projects, shared-build* model for formal mathematics, with agent-run discovery, repair, optimization, and review under kernel-plus-linter admission gates.
* An honest operating record of what agent maintenance does and does not achieve: large-scale upgrade repair works but needs follow-up integration; proof shortening can slow builds; LLM review lacks accuracy labels and was paused.
* Provenance-preserving project cards (human, AI, or mixed proof authorship) and an exposition layer linking informal claims to formal statements — a concrete template for claim-to-artifact traceability.
* A vision statement: most new mathematics papers should ship with a maintained formalization whose imports mirror the citation graph.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Label the reviewer:** have human mathematicians grade a sample of LLM review verdicts (especially statement-faithfulness flags) to get precision and recall, instead of counting merge outcomes.
* **Statement-drift checks:** when an upgrade repair changes hypotheses, automatically diff theorem statements and route any weakening to human sign-off. This matters because the paper shows repairs can quietly change what is proved.
* **Log agent effort:** record per-job model, tokens, wall-clock time, and human intervention, so the claim that agents maintain the archive becomes quantitative.
* **Make performance a merge gate:** proof golfing slowed builds once. Tau Ceti's required performance check is a direct fix.
* **Link to research verifiability (reader-constructed):** Lean Pool is the formal-mathematics end of the claim-to-evidence spectrum tracked in [Research verifiability](../topics/research-verifiability.md). The kernel removes proof-level doubt, so the remaining weak point is whether the *formal statement matches the informal claim* — the same gap that the LLM-judge audits in that topic try to close for empirical claims.
* **Project fit (reader note):** the upgrade workflow (probe everything → assign each failure to a repair agent → combined build → draft PR) is a reusable pattern for keeping this repository's skills and scripts working across dependency upgrades.
