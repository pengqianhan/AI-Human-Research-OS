# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a scientific field, a seed benchmark for that field (100 seed questions with their data files), and scientific literature the generator agent searches; a frontier coding agent (Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.8, or Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol) that plans, implements, solves, and judges.
* **Output (Y):** a benchmark of tasks, each a question, a set of scientific data files, and a single ground-truth answer checkable by exact match, where answering requires analysing the files rather than reading the question or recalling facts, and which current solvers fail.
* **Core problem:** scientific-agent benchmarks are slow and expensive to build and saturate quickly. The paper asks whether such benchmarks can be generated and then revised automatically in response to how agents actually solve them, so that a task cannot be answered by a shortcut such as reading one tool output and instead needs re-examining raw data, checking intermediate results, and combining evidence.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. Automatic benchmark generation exists for LLM question answering: AutoBencher and BenchMaker generate instances from declarative objectives or quality criteria, BenchAgents and Benchmark Self-Evolving build or extend benchmarks in stages or by transformations, and S-Eval generates safety prompts from a risk taxonomy. The paper argues these do not produce agent tasks, which need data and an environment from which the answer must be acquired. Its additions are a two-level plan (concept, then recipe) that decides which information is given and which must be recovered, refinement driven by solver trajectories, and memory of past refinements that shapes new concepts.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

1. **Generated tasks are harder without being worse.** If tasks are planned as concept plus recipe and refined until verifiable and unsolved, solver accuracy will be lower than on human-curated seed benchmarks, while judged quality will not fall (Tables 1 and 2).
2. **Refinement raises difficulty.** If a task is revised from observed solver behavior, the share of hard tasks will grow over rounds, and the revisions will move tasks toward raw-data re-examination and evidence integration (Figure 4).
3. **Experience transfers.** If summaries of earlier refinement histories are given to concept generation, new concepts will yield harder tasks than concepts generated independently (Table 3).
4. **Plans are the right thing to revise.** Revising the concept and recipe will raise difficulty more than revising the instantiated task directly (Figure 5).

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Human-curated benchmarks.** Fixed-question LLM benchmarks for knowledge, mathematics, code, and scientific reasoning (Hendrycks et al., Cobbe et al., Chen et al., Rein et al.); SWE-bench, WebArena, and OSWorld for agents; LAB-Bench, ScienceAgentBench, and CompBioBench for scientific agents.
* **Automatic benchmark generation.** AutoBencher, BenchMaker, BenchAgents, Benchmark Self-Evolving, S-Eval.
* **Seed benchmarks.** CompBioBench (computational biology), MatTools (pymatgen-based materials tool use), MedCTA (clinical tool agents over radiology, pathology, and reports).

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Concept** c = (domain, data modality, required operations), proposed from concepts inferred from the seed questions plus workflows and recurring analytical patterns extracted from recent literature; earlier concepts are shown to discourage duplicates.
2. **Recipe**, a seven-field plan: data strategy (reuse benchmark data, retrieve from the web, or simulate), data plan, how the answer is constructed and hidden, how the ground truth is computed and verified, answer format, tools and resources, and how the question avoids revealing the solution.
3. **Implementation.** The agent obtains or builds the data, computes and verifies the answer with code, and writes a question with a unique exact-match answer.
4. **Solve and judge.** The generator model solves the task in three independent trials; a judge reads the question, data, reference, answers, and trajectories and rates verifiability and difficulty (easy, moderate, hard when all, some, or none of the valid trials succeed), with reasons.
5. **Refine.** If the task is not both verifiable and hard, the recipe is revised; after four consecutive recipe revisions without success, the concept itself is revised. At most 10 rounds.
6. **Experience.** Each finished history (successful or not) is summarized into a card of lessons, useful designs, anti-patterns, and guidance; up to 12 cards and 120 earlier concepts guide the next batch of concepts.

The intended difference from QA-generation methods is that solver behavior, not a fixed quality rubric, drives revision: a shortcut seen in a trajectory becomes a design change in the data. The paper's example adds sequencing errors to UMI barcodes so that counting distinct sequences no longer gives the answer, and all three solver trials then fail.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Domains and seeds.** Computational biology (all 100 CompBioBench questions), materials science (100 sampled MatTools code-generation tasks), clinical imaging (100 from the MedCTA training split).
* **Generation.** Claude Code CLI with Claude Opus 4.8, and Codex CLI with GPT-5.6 Sol, each using one model for every stage and also serving as the refinement solver; high reasoning effort, CLI-default temperature, 3,600 s per stage. For each domain and generator: five experience-guided iterations of 10 concepts, one refinement run per concept, and one task kept per run, preferring higher difficulty (50 tasks).
* **Solver evaluation.** Ten models (Claude Opus 5, Opus 4.8, Opus 4.7, Sonnet 5, Haiku 4.5, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, Luna, GPT-5.5) solve the generated tasks and the seed benchmarks; CompBioBench numbers come from its official leaderboard, several at a different or unreported reasoning effort; human-curated clinical imaging is omitted because MedCTA is LLM-graded.
* **Quality.** Two LLM judges (Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Sol) score verifiability, usefulness, and fitness for agent evaluation on 1-5 scales; inter-judge rank agreement is reported.
* **Ablations** (materials science, Claude Opus 4.8 only): with and without experience-guided concept generation (10 shared initial concepts, 50 tasks each); full framework against no recipe and no concept-plus-recipe.
* **Cost.** Dollars per generated question and the share spent on implementation and solving.
* **Not designed in.** No domain-expert review of generated questions or reference answers; no human quality ratings; no repeated generation runs; no judge from a model family outside the two generators.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * *Generated tasks are harder, especially from Opus 4.8.* Averaged over ten solvers, accuracy is 79.8% on CompBioBench against 57.4% (Opus-4.8-generated) and 74.1% (GPT-5.6-Sol-generated), and 95.7% on MatTools against 70.2% and 77.6%. The abstract's "22.4 and 25.5 points" are the Opus-4.8-generated drops; the GPT-5.6-Sol-generated drops are 5.7 and 18.1 points.
  * *They separate models better in materials science.* All ten solvers score 92.7-97.7% on MatTools, while on the Opus-4.8-generated set they range from 21.3% (Haiku 4.5) to 90.0% (Claude Opus 5).
  * *Experience helps in the one ablation run.* Without experience cards, solver success is 66.7% and 15.0% of tasks are hard; with them, 35.0% and 50.0%.
  * *Both judges prefer the generated tasks.* Averaged over judges, quality is 3.93 and 4.28 for the two generated computational-biology sets against 3.18 for CompBioBench, 3.87 and 4.06 against 2.54 for MatTools, and 3.07 and 4.02 against 2.23 for MedCTA; each judge alone gives the same ordering, and pooled inter-judge rank correlations are 0.74-0.87 (Spearman).
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * *Difficulty is partly selected for.* The generator is also the refinement solver, refinement continues until all three of its trials fail (or 10 rounds pass), and the kept task per run is the hardest. Low accuracy for the generating model is therefore partly by construction. Other solvers also score lower, so the difficulty transfers, but the comparison with human benchmarks is between filtered and unfiltered sets.
  * *The "benchmark updating" result is confounded by the same filter (reader inference).* Claude Opus 5 beats Opus 4.8 by 19.5 points on the Opus-4.8-generated sets and GPT-6 Astra beats GPT-5.6 Sol by 15.1 on the GPT-5.6-Sol-generated sets. Any model that differs from the one the tasks were filtered against would gain on such a set, so the gap overstates how fast newer models improve.
  * *Reference answers are not expert-checked.* Answers are computed by the generator's own code, often on simulated data (the microsatellite-instability example uses a synthetic reference and simulated BAM files), and verifiability is judged by the same model family. A task that no trial solves can be hard because the reference is wrong or the question ambiguous; generated sets score only 2.99-4.08 on judged verifiability, so the judges themselves flag some ambiguity.
  * *Quality judges are the generators.* Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Sol rate benchmarks that they themselves produced. GPT-5.6 Sol rates its own computational-biology set 0.58 points above Opus 4.8's, while Opus 4.8 rates them only 0.12 apart. The fitness criterion (need for observation, planning, tool execution) also describes what the generator was asked to build, so MatTools' low usefulness and fitness (1.49 and 1.39) partly reflect a code-generation benchmark judged against an agentic-analysis rubric (reader inference).
  * *Uneven baselines.* Human CompBioBench scores come from a leaderboard, several at a different or unreported reasoning effort, and no human-curated clinical-imaging comparison exists.
  * *Narrow ablations.* Experience and planning ablations each use one domain, one generator, 50 tasks, and one run; Figure 5's curves are not given as numbers in the text.
  * *Cost.* $22.36-58.47 per question with Opus 4.8 and $110.45-114.11 with GPT-5.6 Sol, 79-92% of it spent on implementation and solving; if those figures are per kept task, a 50-task benchmark costs roughly $1,100-5,700 per domain and generator (reader arithmetic).

# Q9 Contributions

* A framework that turns benchmark construction for scientific agents into a loop: concept and recipe planning, implementation with data files and verified answers, solver-and-judge evaluation, feedback-driven revision of the plan, and experience-guided generation of new concepts.
* Generated benchmarks in computational biology, materials science, and clinical imaging that lower solver accuracy relative to the seed benchmarks and spread models apart where the seed benchmark is saturated, with twelve worked examples and all prompts.
* Ablations indicating that refinement history improves later concept generation and that revising structured plans raises difficulty more than revising tasks directly.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Expert validation.** Have domain experts check a sample of generated questions and reference answers, especially tasks that no solver solves, to estimate how much "hard" is "wrong".
* **Independent filters and judges.** Filter difficulty with a fixed panel of solvers disjoint from the evaluated models, and judge quality with a model family that did not generate the tasks or with humans.
* **Repeat generation.** Run the pipeline several times per domain to see how stable task difficulty and benchmark rankings are.
* **Saturation over time.** Regenerate with the newer models (Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra) and check whether the gap reopens, which is the adaptive-evaluation claim the paper motivates but does not yet test end to end.
* **Release.** Without the generated tasks and code, other groups cannot audit or reuse the benchmarks.
* **Project fit (reader note).** The concept-recipe-refine loop is a usable pattern for building evaluation tasks for this repository's own research agents, provided the reference answers are checked independently of the model that built them.
