# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** an open base model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, and smaller 4B and 9B bases); public question-answer seeds in mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, and computer science; structured public databases in domains such as biology, chemistry, mathematics, geography, and aviation; and a ReAct environment with web search, webpage visits, scholarly retrieval, and Python.
* **Output (Y):** a post-trained agent model, plus the recipe that produced it.
* **Core question:** how much of the elaborate training stack of current agent models (continued or mid-training, several supervised stages, capability-specific data mixtures, preference optimization, RL, further post-training) is actually needed for a strong, broad agent model?
* **Constraints:** three stages only, with no dedicated mid-training, DPO, or OPD. A question's reference answer is fixed before any trajectory is collected, and only correct trajectories enter SFT.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Not a new problem. Agent foundation models are an active line, and each recent report adds stages: MiroThinker-1.7 combines agentic mid-training, SFT, preference optimization, and RL; Agents-A1 uses long-horizon supervision and domain-routed OPD; [Apodex 1.1](2608.23283.md) studies executable environments and agent coordination. The authors argue the accumulation makes it hard to tell which ingredients matter, raises reproduction cost, and leaves "open" models with closed data pipelines. Their new element is a question about subtraction, answered with a compact recipe and its full construction pipeline rather than a further stage. QUEST, OpenThoughts-Agent, and DR Tulu are earlier open-recipe efforts.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If effort goes into hard, verifiable training data rather than extra training stages, then two SFT stages followed by RL should produce broad agent capability competitive with more elaborate pipelines. Each stage should raise what it targets: model-aware strengthened problems raise self-contained reasoning, programmatic tool-dependent tasks raise evidence-seeking and data analysis, and RL raises long-horizon interaction with tools.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Agent foundation models.** Kimi K2, GLM-4.5, MiroThinker-1.7, Agents-A1, Apodex 1.1.
* **Hard and verifiable task construction.** Self-Instruct and Evol-Instruct for synthetic instruction data; Absolute Zero and Agent0 for difficulty adapted to the learner; TaskCraft, WebShaper, and QUEST for verifiable tool-use and research tasks.
* **RL for reasoning and tool use.** DeepSeek-R1 and [GRPO](../concepts/grpo.md), [Search-R1](../concepts/search-r1.md), DeepResearcher, AgentGym-RL.
* **Training-inference consistency.** Truncated importance sampling, sequence-aware masking, and analyses of token-level corrections on one side; [Rollout Routing Replay](../concepts/rollout-routing-replay.md), numerical-format choices, and precision-aware optimization on the other.
* **Open recipes.** OpenThoughts, OpenThoughts-Agent, DR Tulu, QUEST.
* **Key people.** Yunjie Tian (project lead), Jiayu Zheng, Mengyu Zhao, and Di Fu (ByteDance); Yuqi Jia (Duke University); Yijun Liang (University of Maryland, College Park); Jingchen Sun (University at Buffalo). Likely critics: the authors of Agents-A1, MiroThinker, and QUEST, since the argument that their extra stages are unnecessary is made across papers rather than by ablation.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

**Stage I data: model-aware strengthening.** A generator LLM composes each public seed into a new self-contained question that needs the seed's result as an intermediate step (operation A). The base model then attempts each problem with Python but without search. A problem it fails enters the question bank directly. A problem it solves goes back to the generator together with the model's successful solution, and one of four operations is applied at random: A (composition with a further dependent step), B (parameterization, replacing a fixed value with a symbol), C (range shift, so enumeration or the shortcut no longer works), or D (case analysis). The rewritten question is admitted without a second failure test. GPT-5.5 solves every new or rewritten problem independently three times; the answer is accepted only when all three agree and their derivations pass checks, and it is re-established after each rewrite rather than inherited. A separate LLM then solves the bank, and correct, complete trajectories form the dataset.

**Stage II data: filter, select, compute, verbalize.** A deterministic program filters records from a structured source, selects exactly one entity by a criterion (with a required margin over the runner-up when the criterion is an extreme value), computes a derived quantity, and only then has an LLM phrase the question. The paper's example filters planets with rings, selects the one with the smallest mass (Uranus), and asks for its mean density. Tool dependence is enforced in three ways: construction-time tables are blocked during solving, while general search, webpages, and Python stay open; questions the LLM can answer closed-book are removed; and phrasing is checked for dropped constraints and answer leakage. Intermediate entities and values are kept as verification targets the solver never sees.

**Two-stage SFT.** Stage II continues from the Stage I checkpoint. Loss covers assistant tokens only (reasoning, tool calls, final answers). Each complete trajectory is one example of up to 128K tokens, with no summaries replacing earlier context. Both stages use a constant learning rate of 3e-6, three epochs, and batch size 8.

**RL with verified partial credit.** GRPO under verl uses tasks from the Stage II pipeline. The reward is 1 for a correct answer, otherwise max(0, 0.3P − 0.1(1 − A)), where A says whether a final answer was submitted and P is chained progress over ordered targets: a later target earns credit only when all its prerequisites passed. A target must be identified or used in the reasoning, not merely appear in retrieved text, and targets needing external grounding must be supported by tool observations; checks combine deterministic matching with LLM judgments of equivalence. A task is kept only if, in four offline samples from the current model, at least one is correct and the rewards are not all equal.

**Keeping training faithful to rollouts.** Training is asynchronous (eight rollouts per question, weight sync every two updates, trajectories more than 11 parameter versions old discarded). QUEST-style context condensation divides each rollout into sessions that share its reward and advantage. Token-In-Token-Out passes token IDs through the agent loop so sessions are never re-tokenized, and Rollout Routing Replay records the experts chosen in vLLM and replays them in Megatron.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Models.** Axion 1.0-35B-A3B and 122B-A10B after all three stages; their base and intermediate checkpoints; 4B and 9B variants with the two SFT stages only; Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with the same SFT as a base-model comparison.
* **Benchmarks.** Full [Humanity's Last Exam](../concepts/humanitys-last-exam.md), BioMysteryBench human-difficult, SEAL-0, HiPhO, and the Olympiad and Research tracks of [FrontierScience](../concepts/frontierscience.md). Stage and size comparisons use a 130-question text-only HLE subset (HLE-130).
* **Protocol.** Three runs per benchmark, five for BioMysteryBench, reporting **the best single run** rather than an aggregate. This applies to Axion and to the baselines the authors ran themselves (GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro). Other baseline rows come from official reports or from the Agents-A1 and Apodex reports, and the paper calls cross-source comparisons indicative. Axion's HLE scores block sites such as Hugging Face.
* **Analyses.** Successive checkpoints, which the paper states are not component-removal ablations; base-model effect; SFT across sizes (4B against 9B); RL at 35B and 122B; R3 on and off at 122B; three selected trajectory comparisons (Appendix B).

# Q7 Datasets and Code

The paper states the complete recipe is released, including data construction, generation, filtering, verification, prompts, and training code, at [bytedance/axion1_0](https://github.com/bytedance/axion1_0) (37 stars per `hf papers info`) with a [project page](https://axion1-0.github.io/); neither was opened for this note. The text gives token counts rather than dataset sizes: Stage I processes 841M tokens at 122B and 657M at 35B. SFT runs on 32 GPUs and costs 240 GPU-hours at 122B and about 90 at 35B. Reproduction needs GPT-5.5 for reference answers and an unnamed LLM for trajectories. No new benchmark is introduced.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * *Broad scores for the size.* The 35B and 122B models score 52.2 and 59.0 on HLE (59.1 and 64.8 without the site blocks), 35.3 and 41.7 on BioMysteryBench, 62.2 and 58.6 on SEAL-0, 45.7 and 46.2 on HiPhO, 86.0 and 87.0 on FrontierScience Olympiad, and 56.7 and 66.7 on FrontierScience Research. Against the similarly sized Agents-A1, the 35B model leads on HLE (47.6), SEAL-0 (56.4), and Olympiad (79.0). The 122B Research score exceeds GPT-5.6 Sol (63.3).
  * *SFT carries most of the gain on reasoning.* HLE-130 goes 47.7, 59.2, 66.2, 70.0 at 35B and 40.8, 68.5, 68.5, 74.6 at 122B (base, Stage I, Stage II, RL).
  * *RL matters more on search (reader observation from Table 2).* On SEAL-0, RL adds as much as both SFT stages together: +9.0 against +9.1 at 35B, +5.4 against +5.4 at 122B. On HLE-130 it adds +3.8 against +18.5 and +6.1 against +27.7.
  * *The recipe narrows base-model differences.* The HLE-130 gap between Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 at 35B falls from 9.2 to 0.8 after the same SFT; on BioMysteryBench both reach 35.3, and Qwen3.5 ends higher on SEAL-0 (57.7 against 53.2).
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * *The simplicity claim is not tested directly.* No run adds mid-training, DPO, or OPD to this recipe, and no run removes a stage. The answer to "how much complexity is necessary" is a cross-paper comparison with other models, partly on other reports' numbers (reader observation).
  * *Best-of-runs scores.* Picking the best of three or five runs inflates scores relative to a mean, and no run-to-run spread is given. Official baseline rows may follow another convention.
  * *Small test sets (reader inference from the scores; the paper gives no counts).* SEAL-0 scores fit 111 questions, so one question is about 0.9 points. Most BioMysteryBench scores fit 17 questions (11.8, 17.6, 23.5, 29.4, 35.3, 47.1), so one question is about 6 points, although 41.7 does not fit. On that basis, the 35B model's Stage I drop on this benchmark and the 122B model's RL gain are each about one question.
  * *Stages sometimes hurt.* Stage I lowers 35B BioMysteryBench (23.5 to 17.6), and RL slightly lowers 35B HiPhO (46.8 to 45.7) and FrontierScience Research (58.3 to 56.7). Stage II adds nothing on 122B HLE-130 (68.5).
  * *R3's benefit is mixed.* With R3, 122B gains on HLE-130 (74.6 against 72.3), Olympiad, and Research (66.7 against 61.7), but SEAL-0 falls from 63.1 to 58.6, and the largest gradient spike rises from 21.1 to 26.8. The paper itself limits causal attribution.
  * *Narrow benchmark coverage for an "agent" model (reader observation).* The introduction lists coding and long-horizon interaction among the capabilities evaluated, but the six benchmarks are science, reasoning, and information seeking. None is a coding-agent or multi-service workflow benchmark, the setting of papers such as [ToolSpace](2611.05036.md).
  * *Frontier comparison is partial.* Claude Fable 5 and Opus 5 lead HLE (63.8, 63.6), Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Fable 5 lead BioMysteryBench (49.4, 47.1, 46.1), and many cells in Table 1 are empty, so "competitive" rests on the cells that overlap.
  * *Case studies are illustrations.* The paper says Appendix B describes selected trajectories, not behavior absent from earlier checkpoints.

# Q9 Contributions

* A short published recipe (two SFT stages, then GRPO with a verified-progress reward) that reaches strong scores on science and search benchmarks at 35B and 122B, with detailed hyperparameters and SFT costs.
* A construction method that uses the base model's successes rather than its failures to harden reasoning problems, re-verifying every rewritten answer independently.
* A programmatic tool-dependent task pipeline in which the answer and an ordered chain of intermediate targets exist before an LLM phrases the question, supporting both SFT filtering and RL partial credit.
* A stage-by-stage score table that separates what each stage adds in sequence, with a concrete measurement of R3's effect at 122B.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Add-one and leave-one-out runs (reader suggestion).** Adding OPD or mid-training on the same base and data, and removing each stage, would answer the paper's own question directly.
* **Report means and spreads (reader suggestion).** Averages over runs with intervals would show which stage gains exceed run-to-run noise, especially on the small benchmarks.
* **Test beyond science and search (reader suggestion).** Coding and multi-service workflow benchmarks would show whether tool skills from database-backed questions transfer to tasks with persistent state.
* **Reward and selection design (reader suggestion).** Comparing the chained partial credit with outcome-only rewards, and the offline task screen with no screen, would measure each one's share of RL's search gain.
* **Separate R3 from staleness (from the paper).** The log-probability diagnostics also reflect asynchronous policy staleness and context condensation, so a controlled study is needed to attribute benchmark changes to R3.
