# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a project-level research objective plus task-specific data and an executable environment, presented under one of four progressively reduced methodological-guidance levels while the objective, data, required outputs, and scoring criteria are held fixed: **B1** gives the complete method, numerical formulation, and solver procedure; **B2** removes the procedure and gives only the method class and suitable-approach guidance; **B3** removes the method too, giving only the observed data, scientific objective, and required outputs; **B4** keeps the B3 setting but adds plausible, task-irrelevant distractor information.
* **Output (Y):** verifiable research artifacts and a scalar scientific score per task, produced by an Agent×Model system carrying out problem understanding, method selection, implementation, experimentation, failure diagnosis, iterative refinement, and result validation, then checked by expert-authored gates and weighted scorers validated end-to-end in a sandbox.
* **Core problem:** existing benchmarks test either knowledge recall on problems with known answers, or execution of a substantially human-specified procedure; none jointly measures cross-domain generality, autonomous method selection, and end-to-end project-level execution, and none measures how a *single* research project's outcome degrades as human methodological guidance is withdrawn in a controlled, graded way. The paper's own Table 1 scores eight prior benchmarks (HLE, Terminal-Bench, ScienceAgentBench, PaperBench, MLE-Bench, RE-Bench, DiscoveryBench, SciCode) against cross-domain generality, method autonomy, end-to-end research, and a "guidance gradient" axis; none checks all four, and none has any guidance-gradient design at all.
* **Scope:** 60 project-level research tasks across 11 scientific domains (mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy, materials science, earth science, medicine and biostatistics, computer science, robotics, electrical engineering). The paper's main results table (Table 2) is explicitly scoped to evaluation **without external tool access**; this note has not read the appendices that may report a tool-access condition.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Two literatures already exist and ASI-Bench builds on both rather than originating either. **AI Scientist systems** (Coscientist for tool-augmented chemistry planning; The AI Scientist's idea-to-review pipeline; AI Scientist-v2's agentic tree search reducing template dependence; Co-Scientist and Kosmos for collaborative/long-horizon hypothesis generation) demonstrate broad autonomous-research *workflows*, but their success does not establish autonomy, since objectives, methods, or procedures may already be human-specified. **Scientific-agent benchmarks** progress from knowledge-only tests (GPQA, HLE) through data-driven-discovery benchmarks (DiscoveryBench, BLADE, DSBench, HypoBench) and execution-oriented ones (ScienceAgentBench, MLE-Bench, RE-Bench) to replication benchmarks (PaperBench, CORE-Bench, ReplicationBench) and recent full-cycle or suite-based efforts (FIRE-Bench, ResearchClawBench, SciAgentArena, DiscoveryWorld, AstaBench, ScienceBoard) — but each evaluates a task under one fixed specification, so its score conflates completing an assigned workflow with independently constructing that workflow. A third, thinner line varies information availability: ScienceAgentBench compares performance with and without expert-provided knowledge but is "largely binary" and does not separate method selection from procedural guidance; ProjectionBench progressively discloses information but only for hypothesis generation, not executable project-level workflows; ProjectEval varies specification detail but for software projects, not open-ended science. ASI-Bench's stated novelty is the intersection none of these covers: matched project instances (same objective, data, outputs, scoring) evaluated at four *graded* guidance levels within the same research project, which disentangles four distinct capabilities that prior binary or single-condition designs collapse into one score — following a complete procedure (B1), translating a specified method into an executable workflow (B1→B2), independently constructing that workflow (B2→B3), and resisting task-irrelevant distraction while doing so (B3→B4).

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If methodological guidance is withdrawn in the graded B1→B2→B3→B4 sequence while the research objective, data, and evaluation criteria stay fixed, current frontier Agent×Model systems will show a measurable and *asymmetric* performance collapse: the largest single drop should occur at B1→B2 (losing the step-by-step procedure while keeping the method name), a much smaller drop at B2→B3 (losing the method name itself), and little further drop at B3→B4 (adding irrelevant distractors) — revealing that operationalizing a known method into an executable research workflow, not selecting the method or resisting distraction, is the dominant bottleneck to autonomous scientific research, and that this ordering should hold consistently across domains, model families, and agent harnesses.

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **AI Scientist systems.** Coscientist (chemistry, tool-augmented planning and experimentation), The AI Scientist (idea generation through experimentation, writing, and review), AI Scientist-v2 (agentic tree search reducing reliance on human templates), Co-Scientist and Kosmos (collaborative hypothesis generation and long-horizon investigation) — the paper's Appendix A cites these as demonstrating broad workflow capability without demonstrating autonomy, since their objectives, methods, or procedures may already be substantially human-specified.
* **Scientific-agent benchmarks.** GPQA and HLE (knowledge-only, no execution); SciCode (scientific programming); DiscoveryBench, BLADE, DSBench, HypoBench (data-driven discovery and hypothesis generation); ScienceAgentBench, MLE-Bench, RE-Bench (execution-oriented, open-ended ML/science engineering); PaperBench, CORE-Bench, ReplicationBench (research replication via hierarchical or execution-based evaluation); FIRE-Bench, ResearchClawBench (full-cycle rediscovery and end-to-end research); SciAgentArena, DiscoveryWorld, AstaBench, ScienceBoard (interactive, simulated, or suite-based scientific workflows).
* **Scientific autonomy and guidance dependence.** ScienceAgentBench (binary with/without expert knowledge), ProjectionBench (progressive information disclosure for hypothesis generation only), ProjectEval (specification-detail variation for software projects) — the paper's stated gap is that none of these evaluates the transition from following a prescribed method to independently constructing and validating a workflow within the *same* scientific project, nor how that transition interacts with plausible distractors.
* **Authors and construction effort.** ASI-Bench is a large, distributed collaboration: 21 researchers contributed the 60 retained tasks, drawn from a broader pool of 40+ contributors and reviewers across Tsinghua University, MIT, Harvard, Carnegie Mellon, University of Michigan, UIUC, Boston University, University of Queensland, USTC, the Flatiron Institute, Microsoft Research, and AG2 AI; corresponding author Yongchao Chen. Construction cost more than 31,000 human-hours across five review rounds and over 1,100 task-review assignments.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **B1–B4 guidance gradient on matched project instances.** Each of the 60 project-level tasks is presented at four levels that hold the underlying research objective, task-specific data, required outputs, and scoring criteria constant while only the amount of human methodological guidance changes — from a complete solver procedure (B1) down to objective-and-data-only (B3), then B3 plus factually correct but task-irrelevant distractor context (B4). Because the four levels share one task identity, differences between them isolate what withdrawing guidance costs rather than conflating it with task difficulty.
2. **Multi-stage construction and validation pipeline.** More than 1,300 candidate research ideas were collected from traceable scientific sources, screened and engineered into executable tasks by domain experts, then passed through AI-assisted auditing and five rounds of human cross-review (over 1,100 review assignments, over 2,000 task revisions) covering scientific logic, task specification, reference artifacts, scoring code, information leakage, and agent trajectories. Every retained task was further validated by end-to-end execution in an isolated sandbox (over 1,500 sandbox runs) checking runtime stability, reference reproducibility, artifact generation, and scoring consistency; tasks with unresolved scientific errors, unstable execution, evaluation misalignment, or unintended solution shortcuts were revised or excluded before the final 60 remained.
3. **Task scale and richness.** Across the 60 tasks, completing the underlying research process involves more than 2,600 interaction turns and 2,400 execution steps, spanning over 35 hours of agent execution in total — the paper frames each task as a genuine long-horizon, multi-stage investigation with multiple interdependent, open-ended decision points, not an isolated question or a fixed-procedure exercise.
4. **Diagnostic decomposition rather than one aggregate score.** Reporting B2−B1, B3−B1, and B4−B3 deltas per model (computed within each run before aggregation) turns the benchmark into a diagnostic instrument: a system's B1 score alone cannot distinguish "good at following instructions" from "good at autonomous research," but the delta pattern across levels can.
5. **Open, community-extensible task pipeline.** A public submission portal (asibench.apexin.ai/submit) and CLI launcher walk contributors through a 15-step Guided Flow: formulate a non-trivial, deterministic scientific question; build all four B1–B4 prompt levels for that same objective (B3/B4 must not leak the intended method); supply a reference-generation script (`generate_gt.py`), evaluation gates and weighted scorers, runtime dependencies, and local-testing evidence across all four conditions; then submit for the same scientific/technical review the original 60 tasks underwent. Accepted contributors are credited in the corresponding benchmark release, explicitly treating community submitters as co-builders rather than external data providers.

# Q6 Experiment Design

18 Agent×Model configurations spanning six agent harnesses (a default/proprietary harness, Claude Code, Kimi Code, MiMo Code, and OpenHands) paired with backbone models including GPT-5.5 (xhigh), GPT-5.6 Sol (xhigh and ultra), Claude Opus 5 and Claude Opus 4.8, Kimi K3 and Kimi K2.7, GLM-5.3 and GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 Flash and Pro, and MiMo V2.5 Pro — evaluated on all 60 tasks under B1–B4, macro-averaged over three independent runs per configuration (Claude Opus 5 is reported from a single run, with no estimable standard deviation, and is explicitly flagged as such in the results table). The paper reports mean ± one sample standard deviation for the main scores, plus the B2−B1, B3−B1, and B4−B3 diagnostic deltas computed per run before aggregation. A separate cost analysis (§3.2) profiles average per-task token consumption, execution time, and monetary cost per Agent×Model combination under each guidance level, plotting per-run cost against B3 score to characterize the cost–performance trade-off.

# Q7 Datasets and Code

ASI-Bench introduces a new benchmark rather than reusing an existing one: 60 project-level tasks across 11 scientific domains, each with a scientific objective, task-specific data, an executable sandbox environment, a reference-generation script, and gate-plus-weighted-scorer evaluation criteria. Code, a leaderboard, and a Hugging Face listing are released at github.com/apexin-ai/ASI-Bench (245 GitHub stars per HF metadata at the time of this note); a public submission portal (asibench.apexin.ai/submit) accepts community-contributed tasks for future releases under the same review process the initial 60 tasks underwent.

# Q9 Contributions

1. ASI-Bench, the first benchmark to jointly evaluate cross-domain scientific generality, autonomous method selection, and end-to-end project-level research execution in one design.
2. A B1–B4 progressive-guidance-withdrawal protocol applied within matched project instances (same objective, data, and scoring criteria across levels), which decomposes autonomy into four separable failure points — procedural execution, method operationalization, autonomous workflow construction, and distractor robustness — rather than one binary human-guidance toggle.
3. A large-scale, expert-validated construction and review pipeline (40+ contributors, 31,000+ human-hours, five review rounds, over 1,500 sandbox validation runs) that filters roughly 1,300 candidate ideas down to 60 tasks checked for scientific soundness, information leakage, and reproducible scoring.
4. Empirical evidence across 18 Agent×Model configurations that average scientific score collapses from 50.91 (B1) to 29.10 (B2) to 26.62 (B3), identifying method operationalization — not method selection, and not distractor resistance — as the dominant bottleneck to autonomous scientific research, with the strongest configuration (Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol, ultra reasoning) still only reaching 51.60 under B3.
5. Direct evidence that a fixed backbone model's scientific capability is harness-dependent (e.g., MiMo V2.5 Pro moves from 16.17 with MiMo Code to 23.25 with Claude Code; Kimi K2.7 moves from 19.72 to 27.34 across the same two harnesses), so model-only comparisons can misattribute capability that actually comes from the surrounding agent system.
6. A cost-performance analysis showing complete guidance (B1) is computationally cheapest, that partial guidance (B2, method-name-only) is paradoxically the *most* expensive condition (59% more tokens and 32% more time than B1), and that higher spend does not reliably track higher B3 performance across configurations.
7. An open, community-extensible submission pipeline (public portal, CLI launcher, 15-step Guided Flow, contributor credit) intended to keep the benchmark growing and unsaturated as agent capabilities advance.

# Q10 Next Steps

The paper frames the released 60 tasks explicitly as "a starting point rather than a close[d]" collection, and states that no fixed benchmark or single research team can represent the full breadth of scientifically meaningful, verifiable problems future AI systems must confront — it invites researchers "working at the frontier of different fields" to contribute new domains, broaden methodological diversity within existing domains, and cover research capabilities the current 60 tasks underrepresent. It frames current absolute performance as low enough (best B3 score 51.60, average B3 26.62) that the benchmark is "far from saturated," positioning ASI-Bench as a longitudinal tracking instrument rather than a one-time leaderboard. This note adds two connections beyond the paper's own framing, both flagged as open rather than settled by the fetched text: first, ASI-Bench's own "Harnesses Shape Model's Capability" finding is an independent, benchmark-scale replication of a claim this wiki's [Agent harness engineering](../topics/agent-harness-engineering.md) topic already tracks from single-system papers (StateM, LongHorizon-Harness) — that a fixed backbone model's measured capability is not a property of the model alone; second, because the main results table is explicitly scoped to "without external tool access," it is not yet established from the read portion of this paper how much of the steep B1→B2 operationalization gap would close given tool access, or whether the harness-dependence effect interacts differently with guidance level once tools are available — a question the unread Appendix D–onward material may or may not address.
