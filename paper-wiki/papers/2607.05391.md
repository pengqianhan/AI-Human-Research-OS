# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a task prompt `x`, one or more candidate agent trajectories `τ` (interaction histories of messages, tool calls, observations, and outputs) produced by a generation policy, and a verifier language model `p_θ` with access to token-level logits.
* **Output (Y):** a continuous reward `R(x,τ) ∈ [0,1]` per trajectory, a pairwise preference `P(τ_i ≻ τ_j | x)` derived from it via a Bradley–Terry model, and (for N candidates) a single selected trajectory `τ*` chosen through a budget-efficient tournament.
* **Core problem:** standard LLM-as-a-judge methods collapse a model's evaluation into one discrete score token, which produces high tie rates and coarse, poorly calibrated comparisons between complex agent trajectories. How can a verifier extract a fine-grained, well-calibrated reward signal from a language model without any additional training, and how can that signal be used cheaply to select the best of many candidates and to shape RL rewards?
* **Scope:** verification and selection at inference time over already-generated trajectories, plus reward shaping for existing RL algorithms (SAC, GRPO); it does not modify the generation policy's weights and assumes access to scoring-token logits (or a documented two-stage workaround for logit-restricted frontier models, Appendix B.6).

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Verification scaling as an axis distinct from pre-training, post-training, and test-time generation scaling is the paper's framing novelty: prior scaling work (Kaplan et al. scaling laws, test-time compute scaling) targets generation, while verification quality itself is treated as fixed or improved only by training a separate reward model. LLM-as-a-Verifier is not the first LLM-as-a-judge method, but it differs from standard LM judges by taking the expectation over the full scoring-token logit distribution instead of decoding the single highest-probability token, and by explicitly characterizing three orthogonal scaling axes (granularity, repeated evaluation, criteria decomposition) with a signal-to-noise-ratio analysis explaining why granularity helps. It is also, per the related-work framing, applied to long-horizon agent trajectories across three domains (coding, robotics, medical) rather than isolated natural-language responses.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

If a verifier scores candidate trajectories by taking the expectation over the full distribution of scoring-token logits (rather than a decoded discrete score), and that scoring is scaled along granularity `G`, repeated evaluations `K`, and criteria decomposition `C`, then: (1) pairwise verification accuracy and signal-to-noise ratio improve monotonically along each axis; (2) using this continuous reward inside a cost-efficient tournament (Probabilistic Pivot Tournament) for best-of-N selection achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on Terminal-Bench V2, SWE-Bench Verified, RoboRewardBench, and MedAgentBench without any additional training; (3) the same fine-grained score tracks chronological task progress (measured via Value-Order Correlation) and (4) can serve as a dense reward for both off-policy (SAC) and on-policy (GRPO) RL, improving sample efficiency. The paper tests all four claims empirically but does not test whether the scaling axes are exhaustive, whether criteria decomposition could be learned rather than hand-designed, or multi-turn RL settings beyond single-turn reward shaping (explicitly flagged as future work in Appendix A).

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Test-time scaling:** chain-of-thought, tree/graph search over reasoning, best-of-N and repeated sampling (AlphaCode, self-consistency), inference-aware training. These expose oracle headroom (98.9% oracle Pass@K on Terminal-Bench V2) but require a reliable selector — the gap LLM-as-a-Verifier targets.
* **LLM-as-a-Judge:** probability/form-filling evaluators (GPTScore, G-Eval), benchmark-style preference evaluators (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard), rubric decomposition and specialized judges (FLASK, Prometheus), judge-reliability studies (position/self-enhancement bias). LLM-as-a-Verifier differs by verifying long-horizon multi-domain agent trajectories rather than isolated responses, and by systematically scaling verification quality along three measurable axes instead of only improving prompt/rubric design.
* **Verifiable reward / reward models:** outcome and process reward models (ORM/PRM), generative verifiers, robotics reward models trained on large trajectory/preference corpora (RoboReward, Robometer, TOPReward). LLM-as-a-Verifier is training-free and zero-shot yet outperforms several of these trained baselines on RoboRewardBench.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **Fine-grained reward estimation.** Rather than decoding a single discrete score token, the verifier extracts logprobs over an ordered set of score tokens `V_score = {v_1,...,v_G}` from `<score_A>`/`<score_B>` tags and computes `R(x,τ) = (1/CK) Σ_c Σ_k Σ_g p_θ(v_g | x,c,τ) φ(v_g)`, averaging over `C` criteria, `K` repeated evaluations, and `G` granularity levels, then normalizes to `[0,1]`.
2. **Pairwise preference via Bradley–Terry.** The continuous rewards of two trajectories are converted into a win probability `P(τ_i ≻ τ_j | x) = σ(R(x,τ_i) − R(x,τ_j))`.
3. **Probabilistic Pivot Tournament (PPT).** To select the best of N candidates without the `O(N²)` cost of a full round-robin, PPT runs (i) a ring pass — a random Hamiltonian cycle scoring N adjacent pairs so every candidate appears once in each prompt slot, canceling positional bias; (ii) pivot selection — the top-`k` candidates by ring-pass mean preference become pivots; (iii) pivot rounds — every non-pivot-vs-pivot and pivot-vs-pivot pair is scored. Total cost is `O(Nk²)` with `k ≪ N`, concentrating budget on the most likely-correct candidates.
4. **Three scaling axes measured on Terminal-Bench 2.0:** granularity (accuracy 73.1%→77.5% as `G` goes 1→20, driven by rising signal-to-noise ratio, SNR 0.775→0.799), repeated evaluation (74.7%→77.5% as `K` goes 1→16, a Monte Carlo variance-reduction effect that a single-pass verifier at `K=1` already matches a heavily-ensembled discrete judge at `K=16`), and criteria decomposition (single criteria 75.2–76.4% vs. 78.3% for a three-criterion ensemble — Specification, Output, Errors for code trajectories).
5. **Applications beyond selection.** The same score is used as a task-progress proxy (Value-Order Correlation between chronological step index and verifier score on trajectory prefixes) and as a dense reward for RL: `r_t = r_env_t + λρ_t` for off-policy SAC (DSRL on `π_0`/LIBERO), and `r_i = r_correct,i + r_format,i + β·r_reasoning,i` for on-policy GRPO (Qwen3-8B/MATH), mitigating the zero-gradient collapse when all sampled group responses are wrong.
6. **Deployment extension.** TurboAgent is an inference-time proxy for Claude Code and OpenAI-API-compatible clients that dispatches N candidates in parallel and selects via PPT, requiring no harness or model modification, plus a web UI for live progress monitoring.

# Q6 Experiment Design

Four benchmarks across three domains, same protocol throughout (generate N trajectories, score all pairs via PPT, submit the highest-normalized-score trajectory), default verifier settings `G=20, K=8`, three-criterion decomposition, verifier is Gemini 2.5 Flash unless noted: **Terminal-Bench V2** (Capy scaffold, N=5 from GPT-5.5, plus generalization checks on Terminus-Kira/Claude Opus 4.6 and Terminus-2/GPT-5.3-Codex in Appendix B.1); **SWE-Bench Verified** (mini-swe-agent scaffold, heterogeneous N=3 pool — one trajectory each from Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Flash, MiniMax M2.5); **RoboRewardBench** (Qwen 3.6 35B as VLM verifier over multi-frame video rollouts, 500 sampled trajectory pairs, compared against a discrete-judge baseline and three trained reward models — RoboReward-8B, Robometer-4B, TOPReward); **MedAgentBench** (AgentBench harness, N=5 from Claude Opus 4.8, EHR-simulation tool-use tasks). RL experiments: off-policy DSRL-SAC fine-tuning `π_0` on LIBERO ketchup task (n=5 seeds); on-policy GRPO fine-tuning Qwen3-8B on MATH (n=3 seeds). VOC (Value-Order Correlation) computed as Spearman rank correlation over 500 sampled trajectories on Terminal-Bench V2 and 500 on RoboRewardBench.

# Q7 Datasets and Code

* Uses four existing public benchmarks (Terminal-Bench V2, SWE-Bench Verified, RoboRewardBench, MedAgentBench) and one existing robotics benchmark (LIBERO) plus MATH for RL — no new benchmark dataset is released.
* Code released at github.com/llm-as-a-verifier/llm-as-a-verifier (2,180 stars per HF metadata) and project page at llm-as-a-verifier.com, including the TurboAgent Claude Code/Codex extension.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

The core scaling claims (granularity, repeated evaluation, criteria decomposition) are supported by a controlled ablation on 200 sampled Terminal-Bench trajectories with a signal-to-noise-ratio decomposition explaining the granularity effect mechanistically, plus a concrete case study (query-optimize task) showing a discrete 1–5 judge produces ties in 88/100 runs versus 0 ties for the continuous verifier. The headline state-of-the-art numbers (86.5%, 78.2%, 87.4%, 73.3%) are each compared against Pass@1 and oracle Pass@N upper bounds on the same candidate pool, which cleanly isolates the verifier's contribution from the underlying generator's capability. The RoboRewardBench comparison against trained reward models (RoboReward-8B, Robometer-4B, TOPReward) is a meaningful zero-shot-vs-trained comparison, and the VOC metric (0.966 on robotics, 0.848/0.769 on code for success/failure) gives an independent line of evidence beyond ranking accuracy. Caveats the paper itself flags: the framework requires scoring-token logit access, excluding several restricted frontier APIs (a two-stage workaround is described but only in the appendix); the RL gains are demonstrated only in single-turn settings, not multi-turn agentic RL where the verifier would need to supply per-step rewards across long-horizon rollouts; and criteria decomposition is hand-designed per domain (three fixed criteria for code) rather than learned or dynamically generated.

# Q9 Contributions

1. Introduces LLM-as-a-Verifier, a training-free probabilistic verification framework that computes continuous rewards from scoring-token logit expectations and formally characterizes three complementary verification-scaling axes (granularity, repeated evaluation, criteria decomposition).
2. Proposes the Probabilistic Pivot Tournament, a ring-based pivot-selection algorithm that reduces best-of-N selection cost from `O(N²)` to `O(Nk²)` while concentrating verification budget on the most competitive candidates.
3. Achieves state-of-the-art trajectory selection across four benchmarks and three domains (coding, robotics, medical) without additional training: Terminal-Bench V2 86.5%, SWE-Bench Verified 78.2%, RoboRewardBench 87.4%, MedAgentBench 73.3%.
4. Shows the same fine-grained signal is a calibrated task-progress estimator (VOC up to 0.966) and a dense RL reward that improves sample efficiency (~1.8× for off-policy SAC on LIBERO, ~1.1× for on-policy GRPO on MATH) — and ships this as a live-monitoring extension (TurboAgent) for Claude Code and Codex.

# Q10 Next Steps

* The paper's own limitations (Appendix A): logit-restricted frontier models need the two-stage workaround; the three scaling axes are not claimed exhaustive — criteria decomposition could be learned/dynamically generated per domain, and repeated evaluation could become adaptive (guided by the verifier's own uncertainty) instead of a fixed `K`; RL experiments are limited to single-turn settings, and extending to multi-turn RL where the verifier supplies per-step rewards over long-horizon rollouts (finer credit assignment) is named as a promising direction.
* **Research-OS fit:** the paper's Claude Code/Codex extension (TurboAgent) and its progress-monitoring use case are directly relevant to any Research-OS workflow that runs long agentic sessions and wants an inference-time, training-free signal for pausing, rolling back, or selecting among parallel agent attempts — worth comparing against this repository's own evaluator-contract conventions (hard checks vs. LLM critique) in `INSTRUCTION.md`.
* Worth tracking whether the "probabilistic pivot tournament" pattern (ring pass to cancel positional bias, then pivot-concentrated comparisons) generalizes to other reranking-under-budget problems beyond trajectory selection.
