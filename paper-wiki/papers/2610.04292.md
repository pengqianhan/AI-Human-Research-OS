# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a target object label (for example "office chair") and a reference image, with no geometric or part-level specification. The agent works in a 3D tool environment with a catalog of parts (at least three times the parts required) and, in the baseline setting, a tool for creating new parts. Variants remove the catalog, remove part creation, add one to three revision rounds, or add Wikipedia-grounded attribute and functional requirements to the prompt.
* **Output (Y):** an assembled structure: placed parts with poses and plain-language roles, a material per part, declared joints (fixed, revolute, continuous, prismatic, cylindrical, ball) with axes, origins, and limits, and an assembly sequence with insertion directions.
* **Core problem:** existing 3D-generation evaluations mostly reward geometric or visual quality, while an object that looks right may fall apart, lack its working parts, fail to move, or be impossible to assemble. The paper asks whether LLM agents can generate structures that are *buildable and functional*, and builds a benchmark that measures this end to end.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Partly new. LLM agents that write Blender or CAD code (SceneCraft, BlenderAlchemy, Text2CAD, CAD-code VLMs), part-aware generators (PartCrafter, PartPacker), articulation predictors (JoinABLe, Particulate), physics-aware asset generators (PhysX-3D, PhysX-Anything), and brick-assembly systems with stability checks (BrickGPT, BrickNet, BrickComposer, Blox-Net) each cover a slice. Existing evaluations (T³Bench, Eval3D, multimodal-judge protocols) score appearance, alignment, or, for bricks, stability and placement. What is new is one benchmark that covers heterogeneous object types (LEGO, furniture, articulated daily objects, engineering CAD, open-source hardware products) and evaluates the full pipeline from connectivity to simulated operation, with real-world functional knowledge drawn from Wikipedia and Wikidata as the reference for which parts and motions an object needs.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

1. **Geometry is no longer the bottleneck.** If frontier agents are scored bottom-up, they will score high on soundness and design but much lower on functional parts, kinematics, and operability (Table 2).
2. **Creation helps only strong models.** If part creation is added to retrieval, stronger models will exploit it and weaker ones will be hurt by the extra freedom (Figure 3).
3. **Specification beats iteration.** If agents receive explicit functional requirements, functional completeness and operability will improve more than if they receive extra revision rounds with diagnostic feedback (Figure 4).
4. **Open models fail on conventions and state.** If tool traces are analyzed, open-model failures will concentrate on CAD-program conventions and scene-state tracking rather than geometric reasoning (Section 3.5).

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

LMBuild is three pieces, and its main differentiation is that every level of the score asks a physical question rather than a visual one.

* **Environment.** A stateful pure-Python tool environment with 19 tools (list, inspect, place, move, attach, measure, set material, add or remove joints, set assembly sequence, create part, get scene, metered `check` and `render`, submit). New parts are declarative CSG programs (up to 96 nodes over boxes, cylinders, spheres, tori, extrusions, and revolves) compiled by manifold3d into watertight solids. Geometry is voxelized at 4 mm, there are 23 materials in 9 classes, and renders come from headless Blender. Budgets: 160 tool calls per round, 6 `check` and 4 `render` calls per episode, 80 placed parts, and 30 creation calls (60 when the catalog is removed).
* **Data.** From about 368K source objects (BrickNet, BrickComposer, PartNeXt, Fusion 360 Gallery assemblies, Artiverse, and product-level CAD from 27 open-source hardware projects), a coverage-oriented selection gives LMBuild-Full (2,549 objects) and LMBuild-Core (200 objects, each category at most once, at least four parts each). Each object gets a knowledge sheet: Qwen3.5-27B proposes claims about parts, attributes, subsystems, and kinematics, each with a verbatim quote from pinned Wikipedia revisions, Wikidata, or WordNet, and Gemma-4-31B verifies support and typicality.
* **Evaluation.** Twelve metrics on a 0-100 scale. Soundness: connectivity, collision, tipping stability. Affordance: shape F-score, required functional parts (counted against a population of up to 12 real instances), and kinematics (joint quality as a product of type, axis, offset, range, moving-set, and free-sweep terms, aggregated as an F1 of recall and precision). Design: decomposition, aesthetics, and alignment, each from two VLM judges (Qwen2.5-VL-32B, Gemma-3-27B) calibrated against human ratings, blended with programmatic signals for D.1 and D.3. Realization: assembly-sequence feasibility (insertion sweeps and supported intermediate states), material compatibility, and operability, which drives declared joints in MuJoCo and scores each required capability by its weakest requirement.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Systems.** 30: six closed APIs (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Haiku 4.5), 13 open models served with vLLM (Qwen3, Qwen3-VL, and Qwen3.5 variants from 4B to 35B-A3B, GPT-OSS-120B, Gemma-4-31B-IT, Ministral-3-8B, InternVL3.5-8B/38B, MiniCPM-V-4.5, ERNIE-4.5-VL-28B-A3B), and 11 domain-specific configurations (BrickGPT, LegoACE, PartCrafter-15/8, PartPacker, Cube3D+CubePart, PhysX-Anything, and four Particulate-augmented variants for joints).
* **Harnesses.** Claude models run through the Claude command-line interface and GPT models through Codex, both calling the environment through shell commands; GPT episodes allow up to three execution attempts. Open models use a text tool-call protocol at temperature 0.6, seed 0. Domain-specific systems run their native pipelines, and metrics outside their output space are N/A.
* **Main evaluation.** One trajectory per model and task on the 200-object core set (Table 2), with stability checked on two disjoint stratified 50-object samples and 1,000 resamplings (Tables 6-7).
* **Ablations.** Retrieval-only, creation-only, and baseline on 25 sampled objects (Figure 3); one to three revision rounds and attribute or functional prompts (Figure 4, 95% bootstrap intervals over models and Wilcoxon tests over episodes).
* **Judge calibration.** Four volunteer annotators rated 90 objects generated by six systems; 48 items over eight objects fit the calibration, and seven held-out objects (42 designs per dimension) test it (Table 5).

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Supported.**
  * The central gap is clear in Table 2. Closed APIs score 90.3-97.4 on collision and 69.3-77.6 on decomposition, but at most 42.7 on kinematics (Claude Opus 5) and at most 22.1 on operability (Claude Opus 5). Every open model scores at most 16.7 on kinematics and 10.5 on operability.
  * The failure analysis of the six closed APIs is specific: kinematics averages 37.9 and operability 18.3; 57% of failed motion targets lack the moving part entirely and a further 17% have the part but no joint; 54.5-68.5% of designs miss or misplace at least half of the required components; 48-68% of assembly sequences are not fully executable.
  * The part-creation gap is concrete: 95.7% of creation calls succeed for closed models against 51.7% for open ones (Qwen3.5-27B 89.4%), with failures traced to CAD-kernel conventions (for example clockwise polygon vertices) and malformed program graphs.
  * Explicit functional requirements raise functional-part completeness by 10.3 points and operability by 12.0 (p < 0.001), while three revision rounds give about 2%.
  * Relative rankings are stable across object subsets (Pearson 0.937-0.988 between disjoint 50-object samples).
* **Weaker or narrower than the headline.**
  * The text overstates the best scores. Section 3.2 says the strongest systems "only slightly exceed 50" on kinematics and that operability peaks in "the mid-30s", but Table 2's maxima are 42.7 and 22.1.
  * The benchmark's size is reported inconsistently. The main text, Table 1, and Appendix A.5 use a 200-object core set and a 2,549-object full set; Appendices A.3 and A.6 describe a 50-object core and a full split of 2,349 or 2,399 additional objects.
  * Each model-task pair is one trajectory, so the intervals capture variation over objects, not run-to-run stochasticity (the authors say so in A.6).
  * Harnesses differ by provider (Claude CLI, Codex with up to three attempts, a text tool-call protocol for open models), so part of the closed-open gap may be interface rather than model.
  * The retrieval-versus-creation finding rests on 25 objects and a figure with no numeric table beyond one example (Qwen3-VL-32B 61.7 to 34.1).
  * Physics is simplified: stability is tipping only, operability uses uniform density and fixed friction, and nothing models tolerances, fasteners, or fabrication. The paper treats scores as comparative proxies, not readiness.
  * Design metrics agree with humans at the system level (Spearman 0.77, 0.83, 0.94) but only moderately per item (0.52-0.64), with human-human agreement of 0.52-0.73 on seven held-out objects.
  * Smaller models build smaller assemblies, which makes connectivity, sequence, and material metrics easier (Appendix A.5), so per-metric leaderboards reward simplicity.

# Q9 Contributions

* A tool environment in which agents build articulated, material-assigned, sequenced assemblies, with created parts guaranteed to be valid solids.
* A 2,549-object benchmark (200-object core) drawn from six source types, including complete open-source hardware products, with Wikipedia-grounded and verifier-checked functional knowledge per object.
* A 12-metric hierarchy from connectivity to simulated operability, with human-calibrated VLM judging for design.
* Evidence that functional affordance and operability, not geometry, separate current agents from buildable output, and that specifying functions helps more than revising.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Requirement elicitation.** The specification result suggests agents that first retrieve or ask for an object's functional requirements before building; LMBuild's knowledge sheets could serve as the target of such a step.
* **Repeated runs and harness parity.** Report run-level variance and run all general models under one tool-calling interface to separate model from harness.
* **Functional checks inside the loop.** The rule-based `check` report covers collisions, connectivity, and missing declarations; adding the kinematic and operability tests as feedback may make revision rounds pay off, since current revisions fix geometry the agents already get right.
* **Physical validation.** Print and assemble a subset to calibrate the simulated operability score against reality, as the authors propose.
* **Full split.** Evaluate on LMBuild-Full to see whether rankings and the functional gap hold across 152 categories.
