# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** one question written in one of 13 languages (Arabic, German, Spanish, Filipino, Indonesian, Javanese, Korean, Russian, Telugu, Thai, Uzbek, Vietnamese, Chinese; 19 to 39 questions each), given to a web-enabled model or agent. The relevant entities, sources, and evidence modality are often implicit, so the agent must first work out what and where to search.
* **Output (Y):** one short, unambiguous answer (an entity, name, date, or number), graded correct or incorrect by an LLM judge against the reference answer and its accepted aliases.
* **Core problem:** existing browsing benchmarks each isolate one axis. BrowseComp is English and mostly textual, and its trivia-style questions are drifting into models' parametric knowledge; BrowseComp-ZH and K-BrowseComp cover one other language each; XBCP translates documents but keeps English questions; MM-BrowseComp, BrowseComp-V³, and MERRIN add images, video, and audio but in one language. The paper asks how well agents do when all of these combine on the open web: native-language questions, evidence locked in non-text sources, and multi-hop clue chains.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Not a new problem but a new combination. The question format (hard to find, easy to verify, one short answer) is BrowseComp's. What is new is that every question is authored natively in its language with no English seed, that 64.3% require at least one non-text modality whose presence the question does not announce, and that the questions were pushed to be "extremely difficult" through group discussion of where frontier models fail. Table 1's comparison is the paper's own novelty claim: HyperBrowseComp is the only listed benchmark that is simultaneously open-web, multimodal, multilingual (13 languages), and human-authored. The authors frame the extreme difficulty as a bid for a longer benchmark lifespan.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

The paper is a benchmark paper, so its hypotheses are claims about what the benchmark measures:

1. If questions are authored natively, require non-text and language-specific evidence, and are filtered against no-internet answering, then current web agents will score low and the difficulty will come from discovering and connecting evidence rather than from recall (Table 2, the no-internet audit in Table 7, the shared-failure analysis in Figure 3).
2. If the same model is run with different search integrations, accuracy and cost will change substantially, so agent capability cannot be reported independently of the retrieval harness (Table 2's built-in vs. Exa vs. OWL rows).
3. A human comparison will show that difficulty for models and for people does not coincide question by question (Table 3).

# Q4 Related Work and Key People

* **Frontier knowledge and reasoning benchmarks.** GPQA, [Humanity's Last Exam](../concepts/humanitys-last-exam.md), and the Last Translation Benchmark, which test closed-ended knowledge or translation rather than sustained open-web search.
* **Web-browsing and search benchmarks.** GAIA, WebArena, [BrowseComp](../concepts/browsecomp.md), [BrowseComp-Plus](../concepts/browsecomp-plus.md) (fixed corpus with verified documents and hard negatives), and SealQA (noisy or conflicting search results).
* **Multilingual and multimodal search.** BrowseComp-ZH, K-BrowseComp, XBCP (cross-lingual BrowseComp-Plus, translation-based), MMSearch, MM-BrowseComp (whose use of OWL this paper follows), BrowseComp-V³, and MERRIN.
* **Key people.** First author Alham Fikri Aji and most co-authors are at MBZUAI; others are at Mila, Inception AI, Alibaba Group, and AI Singapore. Annotators who made significant contributions were granted co-authorship. Interested parties: the BrowseComp and BrowseComp-ZH authors whose design is extended, and the MM-BrowseComp and K-BrowseComp groups.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

The contribution is the construction and screening protocol.

* **Answer-first authoring.** Authors start from a publicly verifiable answer and its evidence and then write a question that needs non-trivial search to recover it. Each item records the question, canonical answer, aliases, URLs, exact evidence locations (quotation, page, table or figure, video timestamp, audio interval, or map location), source language, modalities, access dates, and a solution trace.
* **Source policy.** No paywalled, login-gated, private-group, or personal-account sources; no isolated posts by individual social-media users; no sensitive information about private individuals.
* **Validation with "fingerprints".** A second annotator checks interpretability, answer uniqueness, evidence access, and time bounds, and may add non-leading disambiguating constraints (for example "the book contains exactly 13 illustrations of cats") that make the answer certain without making it easier to find. A core-team member does a final pass.
* **No-internet difficulty audit.** Seven models without internet access (Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, Gemini 3.7 Flash, GLM-4.7, GPT-OSS-120B, Kimi K2 Thinking, MiniMax-M2, DeepSeek-V3.2) answer all 500 submitted questions. Questions answered by two or more models (54) are removed, and so are questions answered by one model when the answer is judged too specific to guess (23, assessed by an LLM and confirmed by a human), leaving 423.
* **Encoded release.** The dataset is released in encoded form with a decoding key, to keep it out of automated training-data scrapes.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Models and harnesses.** Five models with provider built-in search (Gemini 3.7 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Luna); three with Exa `web_search` and `web_fetch` tools (GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.7 Flash); one with OWL, a multi-agent workforce with role specialization and multimodal, search, and screenshot-browsing tools (Gemini 3.7 Flash). Built-in and Exa runs use an Inspect AI ReAct loop capped at 25 research steps.
* **Grading.** The answering model grades its own response from a fresh context with the question, the full response, and the accepted answers. Re-scoring all 3,384 saved responses with Gemini 3.7 Flash, GLM-4.7, and Kimi K2 Thinking changes aggregate accuracy by at most 0.80 points with 98.79-99.32% agreement (Table 6).
* **Cost accounting.** Total tokens and cumulative time per configuration, with OWL failures kept in the denominator as incorrect.
* **Human baseline.** 30 questions (10 each in Indonesian, Thai, and Vietnamese), native-speaker students allowed to browse but not to use AI, with an optional give-up after one hour.
* **Difficulty analysis.** Shared failures across the five built-in runs by language, domain, modality, and TF-IDF terms in the annotators' English translations; all associations are labeled descriptive.
* **Not designed in.** Model and harness are not fully crossed (GPT-5.6 Terra and Luna have no Exa run; only one model runs OWL), each configuration runs once, no open-weight model is evaluated with search, and language splits contain different questions, so per-language scores do not measure language ability.

# Q7 Datasets and Code

The benchmark has 423 questions. Modalities (multi-label): Video/YouTube 165 (39.0%), PDF/book/OCR 126 (29.8%), Arithmetic 119 (28.1%), Image 78 (18.4%), Audio 45, Tables 43, Maps 41, Plots/graphs 8, and 41 web-only. The largest primary domains are Economics & Business (18.9%), Media & Entertainment (15.8%), Education (11.3%), and Geography & Transport (9.0%). Data, annotation guidelines, and code "will be public upon paper release", in encoded form; the project page is `https://HyperBrowseComp.github.io/`. The appendices give the resolved prompts, tool schemas, model identifiers, harness parameters, retry and error records, and costs.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

* **Well supported.**
  * The headline difficulty is robust to grading: the best score is 31.68% (134 of 423), the spread across judges is under one point, and 244 questions have no correct answer from any built-in-search model.
  * The harness effect is large and runs in opposite directions by provider: moving from built-in search to Exa raises GPT-5.6 Sol from 19.15% to 26.71% but lowers Gemini 3.7 Flash from 31.68% to 22.22% and Gemini 3.1 Pro from 25.53% to 18.20%. Exa runs also use 1.32-2.20 million tokens per question, against roughly 24-103 thousand per question for built-in search (reader-computed from Table 2's totals of 10.2-43.6 million over 423 questions).
  * The OWL failure accounting is transparent: 93 of 423 runs end in terminal failures (51 worker-step timeouts, 28 exhausted 250-call budgets), and even on completed runs OWL with Gemini 3.7 Flash scores 22.73%, below its built-in search result.
* **Weaker than the headline.**
  * The no-internet filter used two of the evaluated models (both Gemini) but none of the GPT-5.6 models, so the screening removed questions the Gemini models could recall and not those GPT-5.6 could; the direction of this bias works against the reported Gemini lead rather than for it, but the filter is not neutral across the evaluated set.
  * The explanation that Gemini is "tightly co-adapted" to Google Search and YouTube is an interpretation; no experiment isolates it (for example Gemini with a Google-backed external tool).
  * The human study is small (30 questions, three languages) and timed out at one hour; humans solve 15 of 30 and Gemini 3.7 Flash 13 of 30, with only 8 solved by both. It shows that the difficulty profiles differ, not that humans and models are at parity on the full set.
  * Per-language differences are confounded with question difficulty: built-in Gemini 3.7 Flash ranges from 13.16% on German to 60.00% on Vietnamese, and the authors state the splits are not difficulty-matched.
  * Live-web evaluation drifts over time; the authors preserve traces for auditing but exact reruns can differ.
  * The text describes Table 2's resources as "mean recorded resource use per retained question" while the table reports totals; per-question figures are derivable by dividing by 423.

# Q9 Contributions

* A 423-question, 13-language browsing benchmark with native authoring, multimodal and implicit evidence, recorded evidence locations and solution traces, validator fingerprints, and a no-internet difficulty filter.
* Results for five frontier models showing large headroom (best 31.68%, 57.68% shared failure) and a measured, provider-dependent effect of the retrieval harness on both accuracy and cost.
* A judge-sensitivity analysis supporting self-grading for short-answer browsing tasks (at most 0.80 points of aggregate change across three alternative judges).
* A small human comparison and a descriptive failure analysis pointing at magazine pages, photographs, plots, and video as recurring sources of shared failure.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Complete the harness grid.** Run every model with every harness, add open-weight models, and repeat runs to report variance, so model and harness effects can be separated.
* **Difficulty calibration across languages.** The authors name standardizing difficulty across language splits as future work; item-response modeling over the existing results could estimate per-question difficulty independent of language.
* **Failure attribution.** The shared-failure analysis cannot tell retrieval failures from access, interpretation, or reasoning failures; the recorded evidence locations make a step-level audit possible (did the agent reach the right video, timestamp, or page?).
* **Efficient harnesses.** The cost-accuracy mismatch (Gemini's native search is both cheaper and more accurate than Exa or OWL) invites harness designs evaluated on accuracy per token, which the authors also suggest.
* **Project fit (reader note).** The answer-first authoring with recorded evidence location and the fingerprint idea are reusable for building hard, verifiable evaluation questions for this repository's own search and literature agents.
