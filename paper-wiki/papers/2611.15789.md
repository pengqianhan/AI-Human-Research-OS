# Q1 Problem Definition

* **Input (X):** a brief of one of four kinds (a prose script with no distances or angles; one to five photos of a place; the first frame of a clip plus a description of the next seconds; or a reference video to restage), a list of places and subjects with ids, and output requirements (frame count, frame rate, resolution).
* **Output (Y):** a Blender Python program that, run on its own, builds a 4D scene (an oriented box per listed place and subject at every frame, a camera track, and cut times) and renders the untextured graybox proxy video that a controllable video generation model would later paint.
* **Constraints:** sandbox with Blender 5.2, eight CPU cores, no GPU, a 4-hour budget, no network, no external assets, and no access to the scorer; each task is run once. Appearance is never scored.

# Q2 Problem Novelty

Video models such as Kling 4.0 and Seedance 2.5 now accept a graybox proxy render as a control input, and the generated video keeps the proxy's layout and camera, including its mistakes. Coding agents can write the 4D scene behind that proxy, but existing benchmarks either score only video frames (real-world video benchmarks, CineRecon), check scene state only for static scenes or short clips (3DCodeBench, Code4Scene, 4DCodeBench), or start from a single input kind. The paper's Table 1 marks VibePrevisBench as the only benchmark that combines text, image, and video inputs, timed multi-person staging, camera moves with cuts, a 4D scene checked against ground truth, and an on-screen check. The novelty is the combination and the two-sided scoring, not the task of writing Blender code, which earlier agents (SceneCraft, BlenderGym, BlenderWorld) already address.

# Q3 Scientific Hypothesis

The paper is a benchmark and diagnostic study and states observations rather than a hypothesis. Its framing claim is testable: scene-level correctness and on-screen legibility are separate properties, so a benchmark needs both checks. It supports this with two cases: a weak agent's proxy filmed from far above the requested eye level still gets 95.5 from the VLM judge, and 31% of annotated actions pass the rules but are hard or impossible to see in the proxy video.

# Q5 Key Solution Idea

1. **One task format for four inputs.** Every task hides a rubric of checkable facts about entities, layout, spaces, picture structure, routes, events, camera, cuts, and physical validity (nine dimensions). Whatever the input, the agent delivers the same kind of program, so scores compare across input kinds. Script facts are relations; media facts are metric and compared after a similarity-transform alignment of each place.
2. **Replay, not self-report.** A delivery must run on its own, render the full video, and reopen in Blender, or it scores 0 on both checks and stays in the average. Passing programs are re-run from scratch in a fresh sandbox, and scores come only from this replay, never from the agent's own renders.
3. **Rule-based Scene Check.** Each fact scores in [0,1] with a tolerance fixed in advance and linear loss beyond it. Facts of one kind form a block whose mean is corrected for chance and missing entities: Q = clip((P − R)/(1 − R)) · κ, where R is the score the agent's own objects earn when shuffled within each place (200 permutations) and κ is the share of the block's facts whose entities exist. Dimension scores are averaged over the dimensions a task exercises. The scorer is deterministic.
4. **VLM-based Screen Check.** Gemini 4 watches the proxy at 2 fps, blind to agent and object names, first retells the clip without the brief, then rates checklist items (only those the rules passed) as no, partial, or yes on recognition, action, visibility, and story. Recognition counts only if the blind retelling already named the subject. Overall is the mean of Scene and Screen.
5. **Construction.** Scripts are written from a built scene, and two blind readers (Claude and DeepSeek families) must recover each scored claim from the prose, or it is reworded or dropped. Video and continuation ground truth comes from a 4D reconstruction pipeline (shots, subjects, cameras, depth, bodies), photo ground truth from scan datasets with measured 3D boxes; unverifiable facts are marked unscorable. Tasks that did not separate the 11 agents (by item-rest correlation and spread) were dropped, and everything is frozen with checksums.

# Q6 Experiment Design

* **Benchmark.** 242 tasks: 94 scripts, 86 reference videos, 39 photo tasks, 23 continuations, over seven scene domains and 33 places. Median brief 368 words, median output 40 s (6 to 60 s), median 408 rule checks per task (20 to 2,175; 99.0k in all). 88% of tasks have two or more shots, 80% a moving camera, 79% two or more people.
* **Agents.** 11 frontier models at stated reasoning efforts: the GPT models in the Codex CLI, all others in Claude Code. A small toolkit tags objects with task ids so facts bind without visual matching.
* **Analyses.** Per-input-kind scores; API cost per task; paired-bootstrap separation of adjacent agents; 18 scoring variants for rank robustness; three VLM judges and a human preference study; injected defects on 12 deliveries; per-dimension error rates; render-viewing counts; ablations on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (rerun, oracle text caption, no images).

# Q7 Datasets and Code

The benchmark (tasks, media, hidden facts, scorer) is linked at https://github.com/vibeprevisbench/vibeprevisbench with a site at https://vibeprevisbench.github.io; this note did not inspect either. Clips come from over 27k source videos filtered to 388 candidate clips before human selection; 38 of the 39 photo tasks draw on scan datasets with measured 3D boxes and camera poses. The paper does not name the source video collections in the main text.

# Q8 Evidence Quality

**Main results (Table 2, selected rows).**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-6 Astra (max) | 85.6 | 55.8 | 56.8 | 70.0 | 85.4 | 77.7 |
| GPT-6.1 Sol (max) | 84.4 | 55.3 | 56.0 | 69.0 | 83.1 | 76.1 |
| Gemini 4 Argon (high) | 82.1 | 57.1 | 55.8 | 68.4 | 81.7 | 75.0 |
| Opus 5.5 (high) | 83.0 | 59.8 | 53.9 | 68.4 | 79.8 | 74.1 |
| GPT-5.6 Sol (max) | 80.8 | 44.2 | 49.0 | 62.9 | 77.5 | 70.2 |
| DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (max) | 80.1 | 43.2 | 45.7 | 61.3 | 77.9 | 69.6 |
| Muse Spark 1.3 (xhigh) | 77.4 | 40.5 | 40.1 | 57.2 | 65.0 | 61.1 |

* **Headroom.** On only 35 of 242 tasks does any agent reach a Scene score of 90. Taking the best of all 11 agents per task gives 80.6 overall; the top four alone give 80.5.
* **Cost.** Per-task cost ranges 45-fold, from 0.73 USD (GLM 5.3 Flash) to 32.59 USD (GPT-6 Astra). GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 98% of the leader's score at 21% of its cost; Kimi K3 is second in cost and seventh in score.
* **Observation 1, perception separates agents.** The best-to-worst Scene gap grows from 12.0 points on scripts to 19.9 on photos. On photos, picture structure and camera account for 63% of the gap between the top four and the other seven; the top four score 66 to 71 on picture structure, the rest 24 to 38. A text caption of the hidden facts on five video tasks raises DeepSeek V4.1 Flash by 14.0 Scene points. A newer model of the same family gains 11 to 14 points on photos but 3 to 4 on scripts. Runs with a failed Blender API call score within 0.1 points of runs without.
* **Observation 2, uncommon requests become common ones.** When the reference films from a low angle, 66% of shots come out at eye level; only 4% of requested orbits stay orbits (47% become sideways moves); half the video and continuation runs have more shots than the reference; objects mentioned only in prose go missing 35% of the time against 11% for listed objects. Every agent films 32 to 41% of shots at the wrong camera height.
* **Observation 3, looking does not correct.** Agents view 13 to 40 images per run; the third of runs that looks most views 3.3 times as many images as the least, for 0.8 points more at the median. In 1,377 of 2,582 runs with post-render comments the agent says its render matches or looks right, and with agent and task fixed those runs get camera angles, moves, and events wrong as often as the others.
* **Validation.** The scorer gave identical scores on all 2,786 deliveries scored more than once. Injected defects lower the score in the intended dimension (all action delayed 4 s costs 9.8 points, removing one person 12.1), but lifting people 1 m off the ground costs only 0.8, so floating people are missed. Agent-level Screen scores from three judges correlate at Spearman 0.94 to 0.96, run-level only 0.73 to 0.79, and the overall ranking is identical under each judge. Human pairwise preference on 242 proxy-video pairs correlates with each judge's agent means at Spearman 0.90 to 0.92. Five of ten adjacent agent pairs are not separated by a paired bootstrap; rankings under 18 scoring variants agree at Kendall τ of at least 0.855.

**Limits on the evidence (reader observations).**

* The task-pruning step kept tasks that separated these same 11 agents. That makes the benchmark discriminative for this cohort by construction; whether it ranks future agents as well, or whether harder-for-all tasks were removed, is not reported.
* The no-images ablation (−0.8 overall on eight script tasks) is the same size as rerun noise: an unchanged rerun of the same eight tasks moves the overall score by +0.2 on average with a mean absolute per-task change of 5.5, and the no-images run also shows 5.5. The oracle-caption result rests on five video tasks. Both ablations are on one agent.
* Each task is run once per agent, and the human study used one annotator per pair as described, so per-run and per-task conclusions carry more noise than the agent-level correlations suggest.
* The ground truth for video and most continuation tasks is a reconstruction pipeline's estimate, so metric facts there inherit its errors; unverifiable facts are dropped rather than scored, which can shift which aspects a video task actually tests.
* The judge is Gemini 4, and Gemini models are among the agents. The paper measures this (1.3 points in favor of its own family, no rank change), which is reassuring for ranks but not zero.

The benchmark's construction and validation are careful, and the three observations are each supported by several independent measurements. The causal readings (perception as the bottleneck, scarce training data as the cause of the common-choice bias) are interpretations.

# Q9 Contributions

1. A 242-task benchmark for agent-built 4D scenes from four input kinds, with long multi-person, multi-shot scenes and hidden checkable facts.
2. A two-part score: a deterministic, chance-corrected scene check on the replayed program, and a blind-retell VLM check limited to facts the rules passed.
3. A diagnostic study of 11 agents identifying three failure patterns (visual reading of space and camera, collapse of uncommon requests to common defaults, uncorrective self-inspection) with cost and robustness analyses.

# Q10 Next Steps

* **Stated by the paper.** Better visual understanding of space and camera, explicit checks for uncommon requests (camera height and move, shot count, prose-only objects), and item-by-item verification of renders against the brief.
* **Test the verification prescription.** Give agents a checklist step that compares each render against brief items and rerun; if Observation 3 is right, camera-angle errors should fall where more renders alone did not (reader suggestion).
* **Perception versus generation.** Ask the agents to describe a reference clip's camera height and move in text, then compare with what they build; this separates misreading from defaulting, which the one-directional error pattern only suggests (reader suggestion).
* **Floating people.** The physical-validity check misses a 1 m lift; tightening it would change D1 scores, one of the two dimensions the paper names as the source of Opus 5.5's photo lead (53.6 against 41.4 for GPT-6 Astra, Appendix C.2; reader observation).
* **Downstream link.** The paper shows video models inherit proxy errors in examples; a measured correlation between proxy scores and judged quality of the final generated video is not reported.
