# Scope

This topic tracks methods that update an LLM agent's parameters — as opposed to its prompts, skills, or memory, which this wiki tracks under [Agent self-evolution](agent-self-evolution.md) — to improve performance on multi-turn, long-horizon tasks with sparse, trajectory-level rewards. It covers reinforcement learning objectives, evolution strategies and other black-box optimizers, credit-assignment strategies across expanding interaction horizons, and the GPU/memory cost trade-offs each approach implies.

# Papers

* [Agentic ESOpt](../papers/2608.17310.md) - full-parameter evolution-strategies fine-tuning at inference-level GPU memory, using a cosine-decayed perturbation schedule and a variance argument for why its advantage over GRPO/PPO should grow with task horizon; validated on controlled Sudoku (crossover at H*=15), ReAct math/DocVQA, WebArena-Lite (27B, full-parameter), and automatic heuristic design.
* [Agent Lightning](../papers/2508.03680.md) - decouples RL training from agent execution via an MDP formulation and a unified data interface, then applies LightningRL — decomposing any agent's trajectory into individually-grouped (input, output, reward) transitions rather than concatenating turns with masks — so unmodified GRPO/PPO/REINFORCE++ can optimize arbitrarily complex, dynamically-orchestrated agents built in any framework.
* [VibeWorlding](../papers/2608.15265.md) - cold-starts a multimodal 3D-world-construction agent via SFT, then trains it further with GRPO jointly across text-only and multimodal (rendered-image) queries using purely outcome-based rewards, pushing an open-source model from 5-14% Pass@1 as a base model to matching or exceeding frontier closed-source MLLMs.
* [ContextPilot](../papers/2608.28476.md) - fine-tunes a context-management tool-use policy with GRPO over trajectory *snapshots* rather than whole episodes, using context/entropy-variation-guided partial rollout to branch extra continuations at high-sensitivity context-editing actions and averaging each snapshot's reward over all of its branched continuations, with a formal proof that this is a lower-variance estimator of the same target credit than trajectory-level assignment.
* [WideSeek-R1](../papers/2602.04634.md) - extends GRPO to multi-agent, multi-turn training with a shared outcome reward assigned identically to every agent and token in a rollout, plus dual-level (token- and agent-level) advantage reweighting that specifically stops a lead agent from spawning subagents that do not improve final reward.
* [Iris](../papers/2609.04304.md) - alternates supervised fine-tuning and RL against live search in "climbing" rounds, distilling each RL round's hardest-solved, most-efficient rollouts back into the next SFT pass as a self-paced curriculum, rather than running either objective to convergence alone.
* [NeoHorse-1](../papers/2609.08183.md) - updates weights with a routing-guided three-stage SFT curriculum extended to routing-guided on-policy distillation, using a harness router's re-estimated capability-demand score (not the served-model identity, which also reflects deployment policy and availability) as the ordering/credit signal for both stages, rather than an RL reward or evolution-strategies fitness score.
* [Multi-module GRPO](../papers/2508.04660.md) - extends GRPO from single-policy, single-prompt rollout groups to modular LM programs by grouping rollouts at the *module* level — aligning calls to the same named module across trajectories rather than decomposing one trajectory into transitions or snapshots — using a uniform program-level outcome reward as credit for every module-level group; reports 7% average gains over untuned baselines alone and 11% when staged after prompt optimization (MIPROv2) via BetterTogether, at roughly 13x the GPU-hours of prompt optimization alone.
* [One to More, More to One](../papers/2609.23377.md) - shows pooled agentic RL on repository-level SWE hides a "category see-saw" that category-balanced data does not remove, then trains three same-origin category experts with Refresh-Repair-Expand (RLOO + K1-in-reward Agentic-miniRL, mastery refresh, SFT on the expert's own verified successes, frontier expansion) and merges them into one Qwen3.6-27B student by label-routed multi-teacher on-policy distillation with ReLU-gated extrapolation (58.04% on audited SWE-bench Pro-618, +2.54 over pooled RL and higher in every category).
* [SLCA-GRPO](../papers/2609.29050.md) - adds a *structural* credit-assignment axis for tool-calling agents: separately normalized tool-execution and summary-quality advantages are routed only to tool-call tokens and final-answer tokens respectively, inside one policy and one rollout group; +2.53 / +1.36 / +9.15 pp over matched GRPO (Toucan / BFCL / τ²-Bench, 7B), with a factorial control showing the gain comes from blocking the summary→tool path.
* [CompoWorld](../papers/2609.33665.md) - SFT on 3K trajectories from composed multi-service environments, then GRPO on 1K tasks with a Completion-Focused Rubric Reward that weights each rubric criterion by λ + (1 − its group pass rate); Qwen3.6-35B-A3B gains 9.17 points on average across eight benchmarks, but RL adds only 1.2 points over SFT and the authors attribute most of the gain to SFT.
* [AREX-2](../papers/2609.38288.md) - supervised imitation of whole multi-round improvement trajectories from GitHub ML repositories and online-judge problems, with failed rounds kept in context but given no loss, so the model learns to recover from setbacks; Qwen3.8-27B reaches 81.8 Any Medal on MLE-bench Lite (with skills in context) and keeps improving through a 5-hour Frontier-CS budget.
* [X-Tree](../papers/2609.32993.md) - changes the unit of training rather than the optimizer: a deterministically mined hierarchy of reusable sub-procedures enters GRPO as the training instance (offline RL from gold prefixes with a depth-scaled completion bonus, WebArena 18.4 → 22.9 SR at matched compute), as an adaptive reward bonus that is active only while the verifier cannot separate a rollout group (ScienceWorld and WebShop, 1.5B-7B), and as the self-teacher's privileged context in on-policy self-distillation, where it matches LLM-written skill banks.
* [GraphForge](../papers/2609.38923.md) - SFT of Qwen3.6-27B and 35B-A3B on 2,169 teacher trajectories over real-file workspaces whose tasks and rubrics derive from one evidence graph, then rejection fine-tuning with the evidence-anchored rubric as the selection signal; gains on GDPVal-AA, Workspace-Bench-Lite, and SpreadsheetBench II transfer across three scaffolds, and anchored selection beats random selection on every benchmark.
* [Context Language Models](../papers/2609.37725.md) - stepwise GRPO for a model that edits its own context: the trajectory-level outcome advantage is assigned to every segment, and a *success-gated efficiency advantage* re-ranks only successful trajectories within a group by prefix-reuse FLOPs (zero for failures, zero when fewer than two successes) so cheaper correct runs are preferred without rewarding deletion itself. Training Qwen3.5-9B on OpenResearcher lifts held-out BrowseComp-Plus from 28.8% to 42.5%, 0.4 points above a summary harness trained with the same recipe at 1.34 versus 2.19 PFLOPs per question.
* [Recursive Self-Rewrite](../papers/2610.02826.md) - plain SFT whose data step is the contribution: one model collects successes under three terminal harnesses, then acts as planner, critic, and executor to rewrite them into fresh trajectories under the general Terminus 2 harness it will be deployed with. Qwen-3.8-27B trained on 11,094 rewrites (plus 766 Terminus 2 trajectories) beats SFT on the 2,001 raw trajectories on all six evaluations (Terminal-Bench 2 pass@3 74.2% against 53.4%), while the raw-trajectory SFT falls below the base model on that benchmark.
* [MiMo-V2.6](../papers/2610.11959.md) - a disclosed industrial recipe: one mixed-task GRPO run per model (1,568 prompts x 16 rollouts, 2.7-3.7B tokens per step, up to 1M context, $2.6M for the 1.02T-parameter Pro) across code, general, visual, and cyber tasks under several mini-harnesses, with groupwise agentic grading that moves positive advantage toward better passing rollouts, length and tool-error penalties, a frozen MoE router, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation afterwards; DeepSWE v1.1 rises from 58.4 to 72.6 during RL.
* [SoftwareGym](../papers/2610.12611.md) - plain SFT of Qwen3.5-9B (language model only, vision encoder frozen) on 49,445 examples (from 3,715 trajectories before length filtering) collected in synthesized desktop applications, kept only when an outcome judge and a process judge both score at least 0.8; a matched-size comparison finds synthesized-application data close to native OSWorld-application data on OSWorld by single-run average (46.77% against 48.06%) and ahead on the synthesized benchmark.
* [ProgramGym](../papers/2610.13395.md) - full-parameter SFT of Qwen3.8-27B on 64,505 context segments from DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 rollouts filtered by test pass rate; Tests Passed on ProgramBench rises from 34.87% to 42.84% under Claude Code while Almost Resolved falls from 3.50% to 1.50%, and under the Program-Agent workflow the gain from training shrinks to 0.54 points.
* [TerminalHorizon](../papers/2610.13166.md) - full-parameter SFT of Qwen3.5-27B (and the 35B-A3B and 122B-A10B models) on 2,852 DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 trajectories from 1,535 synthesized terminal environments, kept by an LLM filter that judges process without seeing the reward and retains informative failures; Terminal-Bench 2.1 rises from 46.82% to 60.67% and all 12 reported benchmarks improve, with the comparison to larger datasets made per trajectory, not per token.
* [Agent-Reflex](../papers/2610.17953.md) - three stages on Qwen3-8B: 54K verified SFT trajectories in which the policy itself repairs, restarts, or follows a (sometimes wrong) user correction after a failure, guided by a mined reflection skill that is then stripped from the input; then noisy agentic RL on 2.5K EnvScaler tasks plus same-model on-policy distillation with the skill card as privileged context; 58.6 overall on five tool-use benchmarks against 53.6 for Agent-World-8B, with most baseline rows copied from their papers.
* [ActionOPD](../papers/2610.19820.md) - on-policy distillation at the level of semantic actions from a generation-only teacher: at each state the student reaches, four candidates are rewarded by agreement with three sampled teacher actions (canonical checks plus an LLM matcher), the best is executed, and all are trained with a group-relative clipped objective and no outcome reward; Qwen3-4B and 8B gain 3.3-6.9 points on a four-benchmark average, about level with token-level OPD on τ² pass¹ and behind it and offline KD on pass⁴.
* [Mosaic](../papers/2610.22174.md) - multi-session agentic RL for trajectories split by context compaction or subagent forking: each session is its own training segment, the GRPO advantage is rescaled by the within-outcome rank of session count without changing sign, and each non-final session adds a type-normalized reward from its change in answer likelihood; on BrowseComp with Qwen3.5-4B it beats broadcasting by 3.55 points with compaction and about one point with subagents.

# Synthesis

Agentic ESOpt and Agent Lightning give this topic its first direct methodological contrast: gradient-free vs. gradient-based agent fine-tuning. Agentic ESOpt's population-based evolution strategies need no differentiable, per-call credit assignment at all — it treats a whole rollout as a black-box fitness evaluation — while Agent Lightning's LightningRL depends entirely on decomposing execution into fine-grained transitions to assign credit within a trajectory, then hands that decomposition to an otherwise unmodified single-turn algorithm (GRPO, PPO, or REINFORCE++). VibeWorlding adds a second GRPO-based data point alongside Agent Lightning, but at the opposite end of the generality spectrum: rather than a framework-agnostic training methodology usable on any pre-existing agent, it trains one specific agent family (VibeWorlder) tightly co-designed with its own purpose-built sandbox and dual-constraint verifier, applying GRPO jointly across text and multimodal queries with deliberately no reward shaping. Both of these RL papers converge on the same minimalist credit-assignment choice — plain GRPO with little bespoke machinery layered on top (see [GRPO](../concepts/grpo.md)) — which sharpens Agentic ESOpt's own open question about when ES's claimed horizon-dependent advantage over policy-gradient RL actually bites: neither Agent Lightning nor VibeWorlding reports needing anything more sophisticated than GRPO's default grouped-advantage estimation, even on long, multi-turn, tool-using trajectories. ContextPilot directly answers the question this convergence leaves open — what happens once someone *does* build bespoke credit-assignment machinery on top of GRPO for a specific action type — for the special case of context-editing actions: instead of Agent Lightning's per-transition or VibeWorlding's per-trajectory single value, it samples several continuations from each sensitivity-flagged context-editing decision and averages their rewards into that decision's credit, with an explicit variance-reduction proof rather than only an empirical ablation. This is a narrower target than either prior paper's general-purpose training methodology — it exploits the fact that a context-editing action, unlike an arbitrary agent action, can be branched from and re-continued without redoing the whole episode — but it is direct evidence that finer-than-trajectory credit assignment is worth the extra rollout cost for at least one class of long-horizon agent decisions. WideSeek-R1 extends this topic's minimalist-GRPO convergence into the multi-agent setting: rather than inventing a new credit-assignment mechanism, it keeps GRPO's single group-normalized advantage but shares it identically across every agent and token in a multi-agent rollout, adding only a dual-level (token- and agent-level) reweighting term whose main job is preventing a pathological failure mode specific to multi-agent training — a lead agent spawning extra subagents that do not improve the final reward. Iris sits outside this topic's RL-objective comparisons altogether: instead of a new credit-assignment or optimizer variant, its fine-tuning contribution is a curriculum-shaped alternation between SFT and RL ("climbing"), where what changes round to round is which training examples get distilled back into the policy rather than how the gradient itself is computed — a reminder that the training *schedule* (what data enters SFT and when) is a separate lever from the topics' shared focus on how a single RL step assigns credit within one trajectory. NeoHorse-1 adds a second schedule-focused data point next to Iris, but for an entirely non-RL pair of objectives: its three-stage curriculum reorders SFT examples and OPD starting contexts alike by a *routing*-derived capability-demand score rather than a task-difficulty label mined from the training data itself, and explicitly rejects the served-model identity as that score's proxy because deployment policy, overrides, and availability confound it — a concrete instance of this topic's open question about composing parameter-level updates with external signals, here a live serving system's own routing decisions rather than a skill or memory artifact from [Agent self-evolution](agent-self-evolution.md). Multi-module GRPO returns this topic to the credit-assignment axis directly, but for a target none of the single-policy papers above address: a *heterogeneous* program of distinct LM modules, each with its own prompt template and (in principle) its own weights, rather than one policy acting repeatedly across turns. Where Agent Lightning decomposes a trajectory into individual transitions and ContextPilot samples several continuations from one sensitivity-flagged decision point, mmGRPO instead aligns calls to the *same named module* across independently-sampled full-program rollouts and applies GRPO's grouped-advantage update to each module's group separately — the first of this topic's papers to relax GRPO's shared-prompt-within-a-group assumption itself, rather than only its single-call assumption. Its credit assignment is also the coarsest reported in this topic: every module in a trajectory receives the identical program-level outcome reward regardless of that module's own contribution, a design the paper calls deliberately minimal rather than a limitation it works around — and its reported 7% (RL alone) vs. 5% (prompt optimization alone) average gains, at roughly 13x the GPU-hours, gives this topic's cost-versus-benefit question (see Open Questions) its first head-to-head compute accounting between an RL fine-tuning method and a non-fine-tuning alternative. One to More, More to One moves the question from *how one policy assigns credit* to *how the training distribution is split and re-merged*: it keeps a minimal group-relative estimator (RLOO rather than GRPO, to avoid shrinking scarce mixed-group advantages) and instead changes which tasks each policy trains on, re-estimating per-instance mastery between phases because a fixed curriculum goes stale as the policy moves. Its evidence that 30.3% of training records regress during an RL phase whose average success rises is a caution for every paper here that reports only aggregate reward curves.

SLCA-GRPO adds a third credit-assignment axis to this topic, beside the temporal one (Agent Lightning's transitions, ContextPilot's snapshots) and the module one (mmGRPO). It splits a single trajectory's tokens by *role* — tool calls vs. final answer — and gives each role its own group-normalized advantage. Its controls also answer a question the minimalist-GRPO papers leave open. Simply reweighting a unified reward (up to 5:1 toward the tool reward) recovers only part of the gain, so *where* an advantage lands matters, not just how rewards are mixed. Like ContextPilot it argues from variance reduction, but it needs no extra rollouts.

CompoWorld reshapes the reward rather than where the advantage lands. SLCA-GRPO splits credit by token role; CompoWorld keeps one advantage for all agent tokens but reweights *rubric criteria* inside the reward, giving more weight to criteria the rollout group rarely satisfies. Its motivation is a failure mode that rubric-reward GRPO papers elsewhere in this wiki (ScienceBuddy's weighted rubric score, PaperGym's per-criterion score) do not isolate: after SFT, uniform rubric averages are already high while full task completion stays rare, so the reward stops moving. Its evidence is also a caution for the topic. The reweighting helps on an internal evaluation curve, yet on the eight benchmarks RL adds only 1.2 points over SFT and slightly lowers one, so the training *data* (composed environments) mattered far more than the RL objective in this paper.

AREX-2 changes what a training example is rather than the optimizer. Most papers here train on trajectory-level rewards or on successful single attempts; AREX-2 keeps a whole long trajectory if its final score clears a threshold, failures included, and applies the loss only to decisions that made progress (diagnosis, repair, strategy change, improved submission). Its stage-wise ablation separates what this training adds (13.6 MLE-bench Lite points with skills and rounds held fixed) from what skills and a larger round budget give an untrained base model (28.8 to 68.2). Its transfer claim to deep research is weaker than it looks, because the comparison models (AREX 4B and 122B) also differ in base model.
X-Tree adds a fourth credit-assignment axis to the temporal (Agent Lightning, ContextPilot), module (mmGRPO), and role (SLCA-GRPO) axes above: the *data structure* of the trajectory. Where those papers decide which tokens an advantage reaches, X-Tree decides what a training instance is, a recurring sub-procedure mined from the corpus, and makes the reward depend on completing that sub-procedure, with a depth factor so longer compositions earn more. Its online bonus is also the topic's first reward-shaping term that is designed to disappear: it is scaled by how far the group's success rate sits below a threshold, so it supplies gradient exactly in the all-fail groups where GRPO's grouped advantage is zero and fades once the verifier separates rollouts, the regime CompoWorld's completion-focused reweighting cannot reach. The controls (random spans, random trees, compute-matched SFT, resource-matched mining) are more thorough than most here, though on the held-out ScienceWorld fold a random tree does as well, so the structure claim rests on seen tasks.

GraphForge sits with CompoWorld and AREX-2 on the data side of this topic: the optimizer is plain SFT followed by one epoch of rejection fine-tuning, and the contribution is where the trajectories come from and how they are admitted. Its RFT ablation is the cleanest selection-signal test in the topic so far, three arms sharing the same 462 queries and candidate pools and differing only in the judge, and it finds that an evidence-anchored judge beats an unanchored one on the two deterministic-leaning benchmarks while GDPVal's 220 judged tasks cannot separate them. Like CompoWorld it finds the data step dominates: SFT moves GDPVal Elo by 65-100 points, RFT by 7-11.

Recursive Self-Rewrite adds a different data concern to this group: whether trajectories match the harness the trained model will run in. The other data-side papers here (CompoWorld, AREX-2, GraphForge) do not make that match a variable; RSR collects trajectories under three harnesses with different control logic (state machines, verifier-triggered continuation) and finds that training on them as is can hurt, because they assume control the deployment harness does not provide (Direct SFT drops Terminal-Bench 2 pass@3 from 57.0% to 53.4%). Its fix is to re-execute each success under the deployment harness with private guidance and train only on the passing re-executions. The comparison is not data-matched (about 11.9K against 2,001 trajectories), so the share of the gain due to harness matching rather than volume is open.

MiMo-V2.6 shows what the mechanisms above look like when combined at production scale, and adds a credit-assignment axis of its own: *within the set of successes*. GRPO gives every passing rollout in a group the same advantage; MiMo's graders rank the passing rollouts by solution quality and redistribute the positive advantage among them (GAR), or multiply the test reward by rubric scores (GRS). Like Context Language Models' success-gated efficiency term, its length penalty touches only successful rollouts. Its ablation suggests why this matters: without the grader, turn counts and token lengths grow until trajectories hit the length limit and pass rate stalls. The report also raises two stability issues the smaller studies here do not meet: MoE router drift that collapses expert load within 20 steps unless the router is frozen, and agents that pass repository-repair tests by fetching the published fix. Its evidence is one run per model, shown as figures, and the multi-harness and distillation stages have no controls.

SoftwareGym and ProgramGym are both plain SFT on trajectories from agent-built environments, and together they show two ways such results can be narrower than the headline. ProgramGym's fine-tuned model passes more tests on average but completes fewer programs, and its advantage over the base model nearly vanishes once both run inside a review-and-revise workflow, so part of what SFT bought is something a better harness also supplies. SoftwareGym's gain holds on single-run averages as well as best-of-three, but its matched-size comparison shows synthesized data roughly tying native data on the real benchmark rather than adding to it. ProgramGym's teacher is a stronger model than its student, SoftwareGym does not name its trajectory source in the sections read, and neither trains on unfiltered trajectories, so neither separates the environment's verified signal from distillation.

TerminalHorizon makes the strongest data-efficiency claim among the SFT papers here (about 3k trajectories against 32k for Terminal-Universe on the same base model and harness) and is the clearest case of why trajectory count is a poor unit: its trajectories average 52.8 turns and train at up to 262,144 tokens per sequence, and token volume is not reported for either side of that comparison. Its filter also differs from its neighbours'. ProgramGym filters by test pass rate, SoftwareGym by outcome and process judges that must both score at least 0.8, GraphForge by anchored rubrics; TerminalHorizon's reviewer never sees the outcome and keeps failed trajectories whose process is sound, in line with AREX-2's choice to keep failures. TerminalHorizon does not train on its unfiltered set, so whether its filter matters is untested; GraphForge remains the one paper in this group that compares its selection rule with random selection. Like ProgramGym it distills a stronger teacher, and like ProgramGym it has no control that separates the environments' contribution from the teacher's.

Agent-Reflex differs from the SFT papers above in where its training data comes from. ProgramGym, TerminalHorizon, and GraphForge distill a stronger teacher's trajectories; in Agent-Reflex every training continuation is generated by the 8B backbone itself, and stronger models only supply contrasting trajectories and diagnoses that become skill cards. Its RL stage is closest to X-Tree, which also adds same-weights on-policy distillation with a logistic confidence gate; X-Tree's privileged context is a skill tree counted from trajectories, Agent-Reflex's an analyzer-written card per environment, added to outcome RL rather than used alone. Like CompoWorld it trains on environments derived from public MCP specifications, and unlike CompoWorld it perturbs them with failures drawn from logged real errors, the variable its noise-robustness result rests on. Its comparisons are less controlled than that design: most baseline rows come from the baselines' papers, the ablations cover only the noise benchmark, and nothing removes the noise injection itself.

ActionOPD changes what the distillation papers here match. NeoHorse-1, One to More, More to One, MiMo-V2.6, X-Tree, and Agent-Reflex all distill through token-level distributions, which needs the teacher's log-probabilities or the student's own weights; ActionOPD scores the student's next action by whether it means the same as a sampled teacher action, so a DeepSeek teacher can train a Qwen student. Like X-Tree and Agent-Reflex it uses GRPO-style group normalization, but its groups are candidates at one state rather than whole rollouts, and only the best candidate is executed, which steers the visited states toward the teacher's choices. Its evidence is thinner than its design: each configuration is trained once, the KD and OPD baselines are not compute-matched, the LLM matcher is not audited, and the larger student gains less than the smaller one. Its Self-ActionOPD variant is the action-level counterpart of the privileged same-weights distillation in X-Tree and Agent-Reflex.

Mosaic adds a case this topic's credit-assignment papers assume away: a trajectory whose context is not one continuous prefix. When compaction or a subagent fork starts a new session, it trains each session as a separate segment and replaces the broadcast trajectory advantage with two terms. The first rescales the outcome advantage by the session count's rank among same-outcome rollouts, bounded so that it never flips the sign. The second is a per-session shaping reward from the change in the policy's likelihood of the reference answer, normalized separately for normal, compaction, and subagent sessions. The design parallels SLCA-GRPO, which also normalizes segment rewards separately within a GRPO group and routes each advantage to its own tokens, and its shaping reward is IGPO's turn-level information gain moved to session boundaries. Its evidence is one 4B model on one benchmark without seeds: +3.55 points over broadcasting with compaction, about one point with subagents, where single-session GRPO comes within 0.6 points while using fewer sessions.

# Open Questions

* Does evolution strategies' reported horizon-dependent advantage over policy-gradient RL hold on frontier-scale models and real (non-controlled) long-horizon tasks, not just the paper's controlled Sudoku crossover?
* How does population size need to scale with model size and task horizon, beyond a single 4B-vs-9B, G=8-vs-16 probe?
* When is it worth composing parameter-level updates with prompt-space skill/context evolution (as in [Agent self-evolution](agent-self-evolution.md)) rather than choosing one or the other?
* How do black-box, trajectory-level optimizers like ES interact with the credit-assignment concerns this wiki tracks under [Post-training feedback](post-training-feedback.md), which focuses on scalar-vs-textual reward channels rather than the optimizer itself?
* Agent Lightning's credit-assignment module currently assigns every action in an episode the same value (the final return); VibeWorlding's per-transition rewards are similarly coarse (binary or a single proportion-correct score). At what task horizon or trajectory complexity does this simplest-possible credit assignment start to underperform a learned per-step value function, ContextPilot's branch-and-average snapshot credit, or ES's population-based alternative?
* ContextPilot's variance-reduction argument for branch-and-average credit assignment depends on being able to cheaply sample multiple continuations from a decision point — does this generalize to action types other than context-editing (e.g. tool selection, subtask delegation) where branching may be far more expensive per continuation, and if so, at what branching cost does trajectory-level assignment become the better trade-off again?
* Multi-module GRPO's own experiments share one LM's weights across all modules rather than training genuinely separate per-module weight sets, and its uniform program-level reward assigns identical credit to every module regardless of which one drove success or failure — does module-level grouping's reported advantage over treating the whole trajectory as one GRPO group hold up, shrink, or grow once modules are truly independently parameterized, and would a finer per-module credit signal (in the spirit of ContextPilot's branch-and-average or Agent Lightning's per-transition rewards) change which of this topic's papers' minimalist-GRPO convergence still applies?
* One to More, More to One's category experts plus multi-teacher distillation beat pooled RL, but with several times the training compute and with checkpoint selection on the reporting benchmark — does a compute- and selection-matched pooled run (same rollouts, same SFT-on-own-successes) close the gap, and does the per-category "see-saw" also appear in the other papers' multi-task RL runs once their scores are broken down by task category?
* SLCA-GRPO's structural (tool vs. summary) routing and the temporal credit methods (GiGPO, SPO, VinePPO, or ContextPilot's branch-and-average) are claimed to compose, but no paper here tests the combination — does per-step credit inside the tool segment add to segment-locked routing, or do the two target the same variance?
* CompoWorld's completion-focused weighting cannot help on a criterion that every rollout in a group fails. Would pairing it with a curriculum that raises task difficulty gradually, or with per-segment credit like SLCA-GRPO's, turn its internal evaluation gain into a benchmark gain larger than the 1.2 points RL added over SFT?
* AREX-2 keeps failed rounds in context but excludes them from the loss. Is that what teaches recovery, or would training on the same trajectories with failures removed transfer as well? And would RL on the same long-horizon environments, with best-so-far score as reward, beat imitation of a teacher's trajectories?
* X-Tree's adaptive bonus helps most at small rollout counts (n = 4) and fades at n = 16, and its three integrations were never combined. Does a tree-node completion bonus still add anything once a policy is trained with large groups and dense verifiers, and does mining the tree from the improving policy's own rollouts (the authors' stated next step) keep the gain or collapse into reinforcing whatever the policy already does?
* GraphForge's judge both admits SFT trajectories and selects RFT candidates, and its own perturbation audit shows it misses row- and number-level corruption inside cited evidence. How much of the SFT and RFT gain survives when admitted trajectories are re-checked by an independent judge or by deterministic numeric checks, and would the anchored-vs-unanchored RFT gap widen or close?
* Context Language Models gate their efficiency reward on success so that the policy is never paid for deleting context. SLCA-GRPO and X-Tree also add shaped terms to a group-normalized outcome advantage. Is success-gating a general recipe for cost-shaped agentic RL, and does it still hold when the cost metric (here prefix-reuse FLOPs) is only loosely tied to the real serving cost the deployment pays?
* Recursive Self-Rewrite trains on rewrites that are about six times more numerous than the raw trajectories it compares against. With the data volume matched, how much of its gain over direct SFT remains, and does rewriting still help when the source and deployment harnesses are the same?
* MiMo-V2.6 pools four domains in one run with 68% coding tasks, while One to More, More to One finds pooled RL hides category regressions even inside SWE tasks. Does the see-saw appear across domains at this scale, and do MiMo's groupwise graders (described for code tasks and open-ended visual design) widen it by giving some domains a richer signal than others?
* ProgramGym's SFT raised average tests passed and lowered near-complete solutions. Is that a property of filtering on pass rate over mostly easy tasks, and would filtering or weighting by completion reverse it?
* TerminalHorizon and Terminal-Universe share a base model and harness but differ tenfold in trajectory count and by an unreported factor in tokens. Would reporting SFT results per training token change which synthesis methods look data-efficient, and is the benefit of long trajectories more than the benefit of more tokens?
* Agent-Reflex's skill cards are privileged context during training and are stripped at inference. Would giving the same cards to the trained policy at inference add to what distillation put in the weights, or duplicate it, and does the answer change for failure families the cards do not cover?
* ActionOPD's agreement reward gives no credit for being right and, as its appendix notes, is maximized by collapsing onto the teacher's most likely action. On tasks where the teacher is often wrong, or where several actions are valid, does adding an outcome reward to the agreement reward help more than either alone?
* Mosaic's session reward needs a reference answer string. For agents trained against tests or rubrics, as in the coding and environment papers here, what replaces answer likelihood as the potential across a compaction or a fork, and does type-conditioned normalization still matter?
